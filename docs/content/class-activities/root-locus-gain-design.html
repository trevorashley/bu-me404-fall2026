Root Locus: Read, Predict, and Check

ME404 pair activity · November 10, 2026 · 105 minutes

How much can one adjustable gain accomplish? Predict the closed-loop behavior by hand, compare reasoning with your partner, and use MATLAB to test your predictions after each round.

Step 1. Set up your workspace

  1. Find a partner and have paper, a calculator, and this page ready.

  2. Download root_locus_check.m into your MATLAB working folder. This helper contains the supplied models and plotting commands; you will enter the gains you find by hand.

  3. Open MATLAB and set its Current Folder to the folder containing the downloaded file. The helper requires Control System Toolbox. Run these commands to confirm that MATLAB can find the file and that the toolbox is installed:

    which root_locus_check
ver('control')

    The first command should display the path to your downloaded file; the second should list Control System Toolbox. If either is missing, ask for help before Round 1.

  4. Put MATLAB aside until the check interval in each round. Make your hand predictions before running the helper.

Step 2. Read what you will deliver

Prepare one shared response per pair, with both partners’ names. Include:

  • Round notes: For each round, keep your hand sketch or calculation and one sentence explaining its physical meaning. After the MATLAB check, record whether the result supports your prediction and explain any correction.
  • Synthesis and exit answers: During the final 10 minutes, write brief numbered answers to the four questions below. Use specific examples, pole locations, or response results from your round notes to support your reasoning.

Read these questions now so you know what evidence to collect as you work:

  1. Give one example today in which a zero changed the conclusion.
  2. Explain why a magnitude calculation alone cannot place a pole off the locus.
  3. Why must a selected gain be accompanied by all poles and the complete response?
  4. Which conflict motivates PD or lead, and which motivates lag or PI? What would you verify after adding either controller?

Step 3. Assign roles and follow the schedule

One partner draws and records; the other checks signs, angles, and assumptions. Switch roles at the start of each round.

For each 15-minute round:

  1. Predict by hand — 10 minutes. Complete the numbered tasks with your partner. Finish these before trying a labeled stretch task; stretch tasks are optional.
  2. Compare and check — 5 minutes. Share your prediction during the class discussion, then follow that round’s MATLAB instructions. Compare the output with your hand work and update your shared notes.
  3. Pause before the next round. Reconvene with the class before moving on.

Step 4. Review the assumptions and hand-work toolkit

Use this section as a reference during the rounds.

Use linear, continuous-time models, negative unity feedback, normalized signals, and zero initial conditions for forced responses. Time is in seconds, and pole coordinates are in s−1. For every locus, K≥0 varies and all other parameters stay fixed. A controller parameter p>0 places its pole at −p. At K=0, inspect the characteristic polynomial before cancellation; zero gain is a locus endpoint, not an asymptotically stable operating choice for these integrator plants.

Write the loop as L=KL0​=KB/A, with gain excluded from L0​. Here A and B have no common factors, and the leading coefficient of L0​ is positive. Then

P(s)=A(s)+KB(s)=0,T(s)=R(s)Y(s)​=A(s)+KB(s)KB(s)​.

For a point away from poles and zeros, the positive-gain locus requires

argL0​(s)=(2ℓ+1)180∘,K=∣L0​(s)∣1​,ℓ∈Z.

Use these construction rules:

  1. There are n branches starting at the n open-loop poles, counted with multiplicity. They end at the m finite zeros and at infinity. Real coefficients give conjugate symmetry.
  2. A real point is on the locus when an odd number of real poles and zeros lies to its right, counting multiplicity.
  3. When n>m, the n−m asymptotes have centroid and angles σa​=n−m∑pi​−∑zi​​,θℓ​=n−m(2ℓ+1)180∘​,ℓ=0,…,n−m−1.
  4. At a simple pole pi​, the departure angle is ϕdep​=180∘+j∑​arg(pi​−zj​)−k=i∑​arg(pi​−pk​)(mod360∘). Measure each vector from the other pole or zero to pi​. Use the correct quadrant: arg(x+jy)=atan2(y,x). At a repeated open-loop pole, use the local characteristic equation instead of the simple-pole formula.
  5. A closed-loop multiple root satisfies P=0 and ∂P/∂s=0. Where B(s)=0, equivalently solve dK(s)/ds=0 for K(s)=−A/B, then retain only points with real, nonnegative gain on the locus. Substitute back into P to check the gain and multiplicity. A generic double-root event at K>0 has perpendicular arrival and departure directions; a triple root needs its own local analysis.

For an upper left-half-plane pole s=−σ+jωd​, with σ>0,

ωn​=σ2+ωd2​​,ζ=ωn​σ​.

A damping-ratio ray makes angle cos−1ζ with the negative real axis. For the standard second-order transfer function without finite zeros,

T2​(s)=s2+2ζωn​s+ωn2​ωn2​​,Mp​=e−πζ/1−ζ2​,0<ζ<1.

Mp​ is fractional overshoot; multiply by 100 for percent overshoot. Useful design estimates are

ts​≈σ4.6​(1% band),tr​≈ωn​1.8​(10–90% rise, moderate damping).

These are estimates, not guarantees for a complete system with other poles or zeros. Settling means the last entry into a ±1% band about the final value. MATLAB’s default settling band is 2%, so explicitly request 1%.

For a stable Type 1 unity-feedback loop (one uncanceled integrator),

Kv​=s→0lim​sL(s),ess,step​=0,ess,ramp​=Kv​1​

for a unit step and a unit-slope ramp. Check stability before applying final-value formulas. For a cubic s3+a2​s2+a1​s+a0​, the Routh first column is

1,a2​,a2​a2​a1​−a0​​,a0​.

All entries must be positive for asymptotic stability.

Step 5. Work through the six rounds

Start each round when directed. For a MATLAB call that uses K, first assign K the gain you calculated for that round. You can refer to the MATLAB command reference below for additional checks.

Round 1. Can a controller zero supply damping?

A normalized rigid satellite or frictionless joint has G(s)=1/s2. Compare proportional control Dc​=K with PD control Dc​=K(s+1). The proportional and derivative coefficients have the same numerical value in this normalized model; their physical units differ. Both terms act on the tracking error e=r−y, so u=Ke+Ke˙. This placement matters for the reference-step response. Treat ideal differentiation as a model; Round 2 adds a pole to make the controller proper.

  1. Write both characteristic polynomials. Find the proportional-control poles and sketch their locus. Does any K>0 make errors decay?
  2. For PD control, identify the starts, endpoints, real-axis portion, and departure directions from the double pole at zero. Find where two roots meet on the real axis and the corresponding gain. Complete a qualitative sketch with arrows for increasing K.
  3. Match the PD denominator to the second-order form. Find a gain giving ζ=1/2, and report both poles. Would the standard second-order overshoot formula necessarily give the actual PD overshoot? Explain before simulating.

Stretch: For a complex pole s=x+jy, separate real and imaginary parts of the PD polynomial. Prove that its curved locus is a circle, and find its center and radius.

Then check with MATLAB: Run root_locus_check(1,K) at your PD gain. Compare the two loci, then compare the complete PD step response with the standard second-order model having the same denominator and unit DC gain. Record what changed even though the poles did not.

Round 2. What does an added controller pole cost?

Replace ideal PD with a lead controller:

G(s)=s21​,Dc​(s)=Ks+ps+1​,p∈{12,9,4}.

  1. Write P(s) and K(s). For a fixed p>1, identify the branch starts, finite endpoint, real-axis segment, and asymptote centroid and angles.
  2. Find the real-axis multiple-root candidates for p=12, 9, and 4. If needed, use the scaffold: apart from the starting point s=0, the stationary-gain equation reduces to 2s2+(p+3)s+2p=0. For p=12, calculate the two candidate gains and decide which event is break-in and which is breakaway.
  3. At p=9, find the gain at the repeated point and factor the entire characteristic polynomial. How many roots coincide? Contrast the three qualitative locus shapes.

Stretch: Use the discriminant of the stationary-gain equation to explain why the transition occurs at p=9 for p>1. Use the cubic Routh column to determine whether the three controllers are stable for all K>0.

Then check with MATLAB: Run root_locus_check(2) and compare all three loci on equal axis scales. Follow the roots as p changes from 12 through 9 to 4. This repeats the comparison in FPE Example 5.7 without requiring an interactive controller-design tool. Record why moving a controller pole closer to the zero can reduce the damping that was available with PD.

Compare locus shapes first. For ∣s∣≪p, Dc​(s)≈(K/p)(s+1), so the same numerical K in Rounds 1 and 2 does not give the same effective PD gain.

Round 3. Can sensor location change the stability risk?

Flexibility adds a lightly damped mode. Compare these two supplied plants, each with Dc​=K(s+1)/(s+12):

Gc​(s)=s2[(s+0.1)2+6.62](s+0.1)2+62​,Gn​(s)=s2[(s+0.1)2+6.62]1​.

Here c denotes a collocated sensor and actuator on the same rigid body; n denotes a noncollocated sensor across the flexibility. These illustrative models show the effect of nearby zeros; they are not a universal model of every sensor placement. Their low-frequency gains also differ: Gc​/Gn​→36.01 as s→0. Equal numerical controller gains therefore do not give equal low-frequency loop gains. A positive constant rescaling changes the gain labels on a locus, but not its shape or departure directions.

Both loops have poles 0,0,−12,−0.1±6.6j. The collocated loop has zeros −1,−0.1±6j; the noncollocated loop has only the zero at −1.

  1. Find the branch count, number of infinite endpoints, real-axis portion, and asymptotes for each loop.
  2. Calculate the departure direction from pf​=−0.1+6.6j in each case. Share the arithmetic: one partner adds the zero angles and the other adds the other-pole angles. Count the origin twice. State whether each branch initially moves left or right.
  3. Draw a small local sketch around pf​ and its conjugate. Does a departure toward the right prove instability for every K>0? What must MATLAB check before a gain is accepted?

Then check with MATLAB: Run root_locus_check(3). Examine both loci and the views near the flexible poles, then inspect all five closed-loop poles at K=100 and 200. Record the noncollocated case’s change in stability and identify the evidence a rigid-body model would omit.

Round 4. Can two roots meet off the real axis?

Consider

L0​(s)=s(s+2)[(s+1)2+4]1​,L=KL0​.

  1. Mark the starting poles and real-axis segment; find the asymptotes. Calculate the departure angle from −1+2j.
  2. Set q=(s+1)2. Show that the characteristic equation becomes q2+3q−4+K=0. Find all positive-gain repeated-root events using P=0 and ∂s∂P​=2(s+1)(2q+3)=0. At each event, report the gain, root locations, and multiplicities.
  3. Use the quadratic formula in q to explain the sequence: roots meet on the real axis, move vertically, meet the branches from the original complex poles, and then depart horizontally before curving toward the asymptotes. Sketch that sequence with increasing-gain arrows. Horizontal departure is a local tangent, not an entire straight branch.

Stretch: At the complex repeated-root gain, factor P(s) into a squared quadratic. Does a complex repeated root have to be a triple root?

Then check with MATLAB: Run root_locus_check(4) to view the locus, then root_locus_check(4,K) at each event gain you found to mark all roots. Record how the plot agrees with your predicted sequence. Small numerical splitting of a repeated root is expected; use your exact factorization to interpret it.

Round 5. Can motor gain meet the target?

A normalized motor-position plant is G(s)=1/[s(s+1)], with proportional controller Dc​=K.

  1. Derive T(s), its poles, ωn​, and ζ. Sketch the locus, including the breakaway gain. What is the real part of every underdamped pole?
  2. A design request is at most 10% overshoot and a 1% settling time of at most 2 s. Convert these into a damping wedge and an approximate settling line. Does the locus reach their intersection? Explain what your graphical test does and does not guarantee about an exact time-response specification. Consider the real-pole portion too.
  3. Find the gain for ζ=1/2. Calculate the standard overshoot and settling estimate. Separately, test the requested pole sd​=−2+2j by computing the candidate K=−sd​(sd​+1). Can a real positive gain place a pole there?

Then check with MATLAB: Run root_locus_check(5,K) at your ζ=1/2 gain; the helper also shows K=5. Record whether larger gain moved the complex poles farther left or improved overshoot. Propose the kind of controller change that could move the locus.

Round 6. A gain meets damping: does it meet everything else?

Now use G(s)=1/[s(s+2)(s+4)], Dc​=K, and require a complex pair with ζ=1/2.

  1. Form P(s). Write the candidate upper pole as s=−α+j3​α, α>0. Find α by setting the imaginary part of P to zero, then obtain K from the real part. If needed, use s2=−2α2−2j3​α2,s3=8α3.
  2. Find the third pole at the same gain using the sum of the roots, then factor P. Check stability with Routh. Is a dominant-pair approximation plausible?
  3. Compute ωn​, the pair’s predicted overshoot, and the approximate rise and 1% settling times. Write both the complete T and a unit-DC-gain second-order approximation. Which response must be checked before accepting the design?
  4. Find Kv​ and the unit-ramp steady-state error. A second requirement is ess,ramp​≤0.2. What gain would meet the accuracy bound? Does your selected damping gain meet it? Check the poles at the accuracy-bound gain during the discussion.

Stretch: Determine the full stable gain interval. Could the unit-ramp error be reduced to 0.1 with proportional control while retaining stability?

Then check with MATLAB: Save result = root_locus_check(6,K) at your damping gain. Compare the complete response with the pair approximation; the helper uses a common dense time vector and a 1% band. Set Kaccuracy to the gain you calculated from the ramp-error bound, then check all roots at that gain:

pole(feedback(Kaccuracy*result.L0,1))

Record the conflict a lag or PI controller might address and one added-mode effect that would need checking.

Step 6. Complete your synthesis and exit answers

Use the final 10 minutes to complete the four synthesis and exit questions in Step 2. Refer to your round notes for evidence and resolve any remaining differences with your partner.

Before finishing, check that your shared response includes both names, a sketch or calculation and a conclusion for each round, your MATLAB comparisons, and all four exit answers.

MATLAB command reference

Use this reference during the check intervals. The helper calls you need appear in each round above. When you request an output, such as result = root_locus_check(6,K), the helper also returns model and pole data for further checks.

The helper simulates 30 seconds by default. If it warns that a response has not settled, it reports NaN (unverified) for that response’s metrics. Extend the duration with a third argument, for example root_locus_check(6,Kaccuracy,120). Inspect the full response and extend again if needed; a finite simulation is a numerical check, not a proof that no later excursion occurs.

The essential commands, also usable without the helper, are:

s = tf('s');
% Define L0 from the current problem, excluding the varied gain.
% Set K to your hand-calculated positive gain.
rlocus(L0); grid on
T = feedback(K*L0,1);            % Negative unity feedback
pole(T)                         % All closed-loop poles
zero(T)                         % Retain relevant zeros in the response
t = (0:0.001:30)';
y = step(T,t);
plot(t,y); grid on
info = stepinfo(y,t,dcgain(T), ...
    'RiseTimeLimits',[0.1 0.9],'SettlingTimeThreshold',0.01)

Only calculate settling metrics for stable cases, and extend the time vector if the response has not settled. In Round 3, inspect the poles of unstable cases rather than assigning them a settling time. To keep the plotted gain equal to the K in your characteristic equation, use rlocus(L0), then apply the selected gain once in feedback(K*L0,1). Using rlocus(K*L0) would sweep an additional multiplier and relabel the gains.
