Problem #1: Short Answers

Answer each question in at most three sentences or a few lines of algebra. The exam will contain five questions of this kind, chosen from this pool and reworded.

(a) Name two things feedback provides compared with open-loop control and two things it costs. See Feedback Properties, §7.3.

(b) The closed-loop pole pair of a zero-free second-order system, at −1±2j, is moved radially outward, along the same line through the origin, to −2±4j. State what happens to the rise time, the overshoot, and the 1% settling time, and why.

(c) A stable transfer function has a zero in the right half-plane: H(s)=(1−zs​)H0​(s) with z>0, where H0​ is a zero-free second-order transfer function with positive DC gain. Use the derivative decomposition H(s)=H0​(s)−z1​sH0​(s) to explain why the step response initially moves in the wrong direction.

(d) State the condition under which the Final Value Theorem applies. For Y(s)=s(s2+9)3​, the limit lims→0​sY(s)=1/3 exists. Explain why it is not the final value of y(t), and say what y(t) actually does.

(e) A unity-feedback loop is Type 1. The plant gain then drifts by 20%, with the loop still stable. Explain why the steady-state error to a step is still zero while the steady-state error to a ramp changes.

(f) A disturbance W enters at the plant input. One loop has the integrator in the controller Dc​; another has it in the plant G. Which loop rejects a constant disturbance, and why does only one of the two integrators count?

(g) Write the identity relating the sensitivity function S and the complementary sensitivity T. Use it to explain why a loop cannot reject disturbances at the plant output and sensor noise well at the same frequency.

(h) Explain why the Ziegler–Nichols ultimate-gain method cannot be applied to the plant G(s)=(s+1)(s+2)1​ under proportional control. What property of a plant does the method require?

(i) Give one reason derivative action is not used on its own for setpoint tracking, and one reason the derivative term of a practical PID controller is filtered.

(j) The stable system G(s)=s+22​ is driven by u(t)=4cos2t. Find the steady-state output yss​(t), and state why the word steady-state is needed.

Problem #2: Magnetic Levitation, From Physics to a Transfer Function

Source: this is the physical model behind the plant K0​/(s2−a2) of Problem Set 3, Problem 3 (Franklin, Powell, and Emami-Naeini, 8th ed., Problem 3.56; 7th ed., Problem 3.57).

A steel ball of mass m hangs below an electromagnet. Let x be the gap between the magnet and the ball, measured downward so that x increases when the ball falls, and let i be the coil current. The magnetic force on the ball is upward with magnitude ki2/x2, so Newton’s law gives

mx¨=mg−x2ki2​.

Use m=0.1 kg, k=10−4 N⋅m2/A2, and g=10 m/s2, and take the desired operating gap to be x0​=0.05 m.

(a) Find the constant current i0​>0 that holds the ball in equilibrium at x0​.

(b) Write x=x0​+δx and i=i0​+δi, and linearize the equation of motion about the equilibrium. Show the two partial derivatives you need and their numerical values, and write the linear equation for δx. In one sentence, say what has been thrown away and when the linear model is trustworthy.

(c) Find the transfer function δX(s)/δI(s) and its poles, and determine whether the open-loop plant is stable. Interpret the sign of the gain and the location of the unstable pole physically: if the ball is displaced 1 mm and released from rest with the current held at i0​, how long does the linear model say the displacement takes to double for the first time, and what does the doubling interval approach once the growing mode dominates?

(d) Define the control input as u=−δi so that the plant gain is positive, and identify K0​ and a in the Problem Set 3 form K0​/(s2−a2). Show that proportional feedback u=−KP​δx cannot make the ball asymptotically stable for any value of KP​, and describe what the closed-loop poles do as KP​ increases.

(e) Now use δi=KP​δx+KD​δx˙, which requires a measurement of the ball’s velocity. Find the closed-loop characteristic polynomial and the conditions on KP​ and KD​ for stability. Then choose KP​ and KD​ to place the closed-loop poles at −20±20j.

What the exam may change. A different physical system, such as the pendulum about either equilibrium, a tank with a nonlinear outflow, or a different choice of output; or a different equilibrium gap. The steps stay the same: equilibrium, partial derivatives, transfer function, poles, and what feedback must add.

Problem #4: Laplace Solution of Initial-Value Problems and the Final Value Theorem

Solve each initial-value problem for t≥0 with the unilateral Laplace transform, showing the initial-condition terms explicitly when you transform derivatives. Use the cover-up method for simple poles and coefficient matching for the rest. For each part:

  1. Predict: identify the natural poles from the homogeneous equation and say whether the natural response decays, persists, or grows. Distinguish them from the poles that the forcing introduces into Y(s).
  2. Solve: show Y(s), the partial-fraction expansion, and the real-valued y(t).
  3. Verify: check the initial conditions, and check the Final Value Theorem: state whether its hypothesis holds, compute the formal limit lims→0​sY(s), and say whether that number is the final value.

(a) y¨​+3y˙​+2y=4⋅1(t), with y(0)=1 and y˙​(0)=0.

(b) y¨​+4y=8⋅1(t), with y(0)=0 and y˙​(0)=2.

(c) y˙​+2y=2r(t) with r(t)=t and y(0)=1: a first-order system with unit DC gain driven by a unit ramp. After you find y(t), find limt→∞​[y(t)−r(t)] and interpret the result as a tracking lag, both as an offset and as a time delay. Which term of the partial-fraction expansion of Y(s) is responsible for the lag?

What the exam may change. The coefficients, the initial conditions, or the forcing (step, ramp, or sinusoid), chosen so that the poles are still simple to find by hand. The FVT trap in (b), a limit that exists when the final value does not, may appear in a short-answer question instead.
