(Part B) Dubin’s Dynamics

(a) Newton’s and Euler’s laws in the rotating body frame.

Write the velocity as v=vx​exb​​+vy​eyb​​. Since e˙xb​​=ωeyb​​,e˙yb​​=−ωexb​​, the inertial acceleration is a=(v˙x​−ωvy​)exb​​+(v˙y​+ωvx​)eyb​​. Applying Newton’s law along the body axes and Euler’s law about the center of mass gives m(v˙x​−ωvy​)=F,m(v˙y​+ωvx​)=0,Jω˙=M. Therefore, v˙x​=mF​+ωvy​,v˙y​=−ωvx​,ω˙=JM​.​ The velocity-coupling terms arise from the rotating coordinates, even though there is no applied lateral force.

(b) Full state model.

Rotate both body-frame velocity components into the inertial frame: [x˙y˙​​]=[cosθsinθ​−sinθcosθ​][vx​vy​​]. For q=(x,y,θ,vx​,vy​,ω)T and u=(F,M)T, q˙​=​vx​cosθ−vy​sinθvx​sinθ+vy​cosθωF/m+ωvy​−ωvx​M/J​​.​ Part A’s position equations are recovered instantaneously when vy​=0 and vx​=v. However, vy​=0 generally does not remain true in this slipping model: if ωvx​=0, then v˙y​=0. Maintaining no slip during a turn would require a lateral constraint force.

(c) Nonlinearity and inertia coupling

The nonlinear terms are the configuration-dependent factors cosθ2​ multiplying accelerations, the quadratic velocity terms multiplied by sinθ2​, and the trigonometric gravity terms.

The off-diagonal entries represent inertial coupling: accelerating either joint generally requires torque at the other joint because both coordinates affect link 2’s motion. In particular, changing θ1​ translates the elbow and rotates link 2, while changing θ2​ changes link 2’s motion relative to link 1.

Symmetry follows directly from M12​=M21​=D+Bcosθ2​.​ It also follows because M is the Hessian of T with respect to the generalized velocities. As a useful check, detM=AD−B2cos2θ2​≥AD−B2, and expanding the lower bound gives AD−B2=​J1​J2​+J1​m2​lc22​+J2​m2​l12​+J2​m1​lc12​+m1​m2​lc12​lc22​>0.​ The term m22​l12​lc22​ cancels exactly. For positive masses and link inertias Ji​>0, the remaining terms establish that detM>0 at every configuration. Together with M22​=D>0, this proves that M is positive definite.

(d) Linearization about the upward equilibrium

At θ1​=π/2, θ2​=0, and θ˙=0, both gravity components vanish. With τ=0, the equations therefore permit θ¨=0: this is an equilibrium.

Let φ1​=θ1​−π/2 and φ2​=θ2​. To first order, cosθ2​≈1,cosθ1​≈−φ1​,cos(θ1​+θ2​)≈−(φ1​+φ2​). Hence M0​φ¨​+Kφ=τ,​ where M0​=[A+D+2BD+B​D+BD​]=[J1​+m1​lc12​+J2​+m2​(l1​+lc2​)2J2​+m2​lc2​(l1​+lc2​)​J2​+m2​lc2​(l1​+lc2​)J2​+m2​lc22​​],​ K=−[P+QQ​QQ​]=−g[m1​lc1​+m2​l1​+m2​lc2​m2​lc2​​m2​lc2​m2​lc2​​].​ There is no first-order contribution from Cθ˙: it is quadratic in velocities about a rest equilibrium. At this particular equilibrium, the additional factor sinθ2​≈φ2​ makes its leading contribution third order in small deviations. Variations of M also contribute no linear correction beyond M0​φ¨​.

The negative stiffness is physically appropriate: gravity drives a small displacement away from the upward configuration. For P,Q>0, K is negative definite. Since M0​ is positive definite, M0−1​(−K) has positive eigenvalues μi​, giving linearized poles s=±μi​​. The positive real poles establish instability.

(e) Four transfer functions and characteristic polynomial

The outputs are the angular deviations φ1​,φ2​, and the inputs are τ1​,τ2​. With zero initial deviations and velocities, [(A+D+2B)s2−(P+Q)(D+B)s2−Q​(D+B)s2−QDs2−Q​][Φ1​Φ2​​]=[T1​T2​​]. The common characteristic polynomial is χ(s)​=det(M0​s2+K)=[(A+D+2B)s2−(P+Q)](Ds2−Q)−[(D+B)s2−Q]2=(AD−B2)s4−(DP+AQ)s2+PQ.​​ The monic state-space characteristic polynomial is χ(s)/(AD−B2). Inverting the 2×2 matrix gives all four transfer functions, with the other input set to zero in each ratio: T1​Φ1​​=χ(s)Ds2−Q​,T2​Φ1​​=χ(s)Q−(D+B)s2​,​ T1​Φ2​​=χ(s)Q−(D+B)s2​,T2​Φ2​​=χ(s)(A+D+2B)s2−(P+Q)​.​ The equality of the cross transfer functions follows from the symmetric mass and stiffness matrices. To locate the poles, treat χ(s) as a quadratic in s2. Its discriminant is (DP+AQ)2−4(AD−B2)PQ=(DP−AQ)2+4B2PQ>0 for B,P,Q>0. The two roots in s2 are therefore real and distinct. Their sum (DP+AQ)/(AD−B2) and product PQ/(AD−B2) are positive, so both roots are positive. Thus the poles form two distinct real pairs ±λ1​,±λ2​, with λi​>0, consistent with the upward equilibrium’s instability.

(a) Free-body diagrams and equations of motion

Define the signed coupling force on cart 1 by Fc​=k2​(x2​−x1​)+b(x˙2​−x˙1​). Cart 2 experiences the opposite force, −Fc​. The arrows below indicate positive directions for the labeled signed quantities; a negative value reverses the force. The vertical forces satisfy Ni​=mi​g.

Their extension and extension rate depend only on relative displacement and relative velocity, respectively; translating both carts equally does not deform either coupling element.

(b) Transfer functions, characteristic polynomial, and zeros

Define A1​(s)=m1​s2+bs+k1​+k2​,A2​(s)=m2​s2+bs+k2​+k3​,H(s)=bs+k2​. With zero initial conditions, the transformed equations are [A1​−H​−HA2​​][X1​X2​​]=[U0​]. Thus U(s)X1​(s)​=Δ(s)A2​(s)​,U(s)X2​(s)​=Δ(s)H(s)​,​ where Δ(s)​=A1​(s)A2​(s)−H(s)2=m1​m2​s4+b(m1​+m2​)s3+[m1​(k2​+k3​)+m2​(k1​+k2​)]s2+b(k1​+k3​)s+k1​k2​+k1​k3​+k2​k3​.​​ For generic parameters without pole-zero cancellations, the two finite zeros of X1​/U are z1,±​=2m2​−b±b2−4m2​(k2​+k3​)​​.​ This formula also applies at b=0, when z1,±​=±i(k2​+k3​)/m2​​. For b>0, the single finite zero of X2​/U is z2​=−bk2​​.​ The zeros of X1​/U are the roots of A2​(s), the characteristic polynomial of cart 2 with cart 1 clamped at x1​=0. At such a root s=z, an exponential motion of cart 2 can balance an applied force on cart 1 through U=−H(z)X2​, while X1​=0. For b=0, this is the familiar tuned-absorber cancellation at the real forcing frequency (k2​+k3​)/m2​​; for b>0, the zeros lie off the imaginary axis and do not give exact cancellation of a sustained sinusoidal input.

The numerator of X2​/U is the coupling force per unit displacement of cart 1, bs+k2​, which is only first order. For b>0 and an exponential motion at s=−k2​/b, the coupling spring and damper forces cancel, so cart 2 can remain still.

This also explains the generic relative degrees: force acts directly on cart 1’s acceleration, giving relative degree two, while transmission through the damper to cart 2 gives relative degree three for b>0. If b=0 and k2​>0, X2​/U has a constant numerator, no finite zeros, and relative degree four. Exceptional common numerator/denominator factors must be canceled before counting transfer-function zeros.

The reason is that equal displacement no longer produces equal acceleration. For case 1 at t=0, x¨1​(0)=−10 m/s2,x¨2​(0)=−310​ m/s2. Although the damper initially has zero force, relative velocity soon develops and it begins dissipating energy. A nonzero common oscillation therefore exists precisely when k1​/m1​=k3​/m2​; equality of masses and outer springs is one way to satisfy that condition. Linearity and superposition still hold after changing the mass, but the simple in-phase/out-of-phase modal separation does not.

With zero initial conditions and a unit step force, U(s)=1/s, so X1​(s)=sΔ3​(s)3s2+0.5s+15​,X2​(s)=sΔ3​(s)0.5s+5​. Mass and damping affect the transient but not this static balance.

Numerical integration through t=600 s gives x1​≈0.075000 m and x2​≈0.025000 m, each within 10−6 m of its predicted limit. The longer time window is useful because of the slowly decaying pole pair.

For comparison, the final value theorem would not apply to the original equal-mass step responses: their undamped poles at ±i10​ produce persistent oscillation, even though the same static force-balance solution exists.
