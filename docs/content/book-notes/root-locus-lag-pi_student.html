The Root-Locus Design Method IV — Lag, PI, and Design Extensions

Student lecture notes — FPE 8th ed., Sections 5.4.2–5.4.3 and 5.6.2; PI supplement

Lead and PD mainly address transient behavior. Lag and PI address low-frequency tracking and disturbance rejection. They add dynamics, so their benefit must be checked against changes in the transient response.

Prerequisites: III — PD and lead design, system type and error constants, and PID implementation.

Sources: FPE 8th ed., §§5.4.2–5.4.3 (Eqs. 5.72–5.76, Fig. 5.28, and the lag design procedure), checked against the 7th ed. (pp. 270–274); §5.6.2 Example 5.15 for successive loop closure. Nise 7th ed., §9.2, supplies the explicit PI versus lag comparison and Fig. 9.3’s angle argument, with §§9.4–9.5 for combined and feedback compensation. Ogata 5th ed., §§6–7 and 6–8, supplements lag and lag–lead design. Our motor lag and PI designs are [course examples]; §7 uses FPE’s own lead–lag values and §8 uses FPE’s own flexible plant and notch. The 10 rad/s notch calculation in §8 and the rate-feedback numbers in §9 are [course extensions].

Learning objectives

  1. Design a lag pole-zero pair for a specified error-constant improvement.
  2. Derive the characteristic polynomial, stability condition, and error constants of the motor with a lag or PI compensator.
  3. Design a PI controller and verify its increased system type and stability.
  4. Check the slow mode in both reference and disturbance responses.
  5. Explain the roles of lag–lead, notch, and feedback compensation.
  6. Recognize where a pole-zero cancellation or an approximate design argument needs further validation.

Notation and assumptions

Negative unity position feedback, normalized G=1/[s(s+1)], and seconds throughout. Where a formula is derived in general, the motor is G=1/[s(s+a)] with a>0, and a=1 in every numerical example. Plant-input disturbance W adds to the actuator command, so Y=G(U+W). With R=0, E=−Y. Use L=Dc​G, S=1/(1+L), T=L/(1+L). In Dc​=K(s+z)/(s+p), z,p>0 are positive parameters and the actual locations are −z,−p. Lag has z>p; PI has p=0 and z>0. Settling times use the course’s 1% band.

Where we are, and where this lecture goes

Lecture III fixed the transient by adding a zero that pulled the branches left. Lecture II left a second problem untouched: the tracking error to a ramp, which is set by the loop gain at low frequency and which the locus does not display. This lecture addresses that problem, and the trick is almost the opposite of lead. Instead of moving the dominant branches, we try to leave them exactly where they are.

The device is a pole and a zero placed very close together near the origin. Seen from the dominant pair, the two arrows nearly coincide, so their angles cancel and the locus near the pair hardly moves. Seen from s=0, their ratio z/p multiplies the DC gain of the controller, and with it the velocity constant. Such a pair is a lag compensator; the name refers to the negative phase it contributes, which Part V will make precise. Push the pole all the way to the origin and the finite DC gain becomes an integrator: the lag becomes PI, and the system type goes up by one.

Nothing is free. The added pole and zero capture one closed-loop pole near the origin, and that pole is slow. The story of this lecture is stating the accuracy requirement (§1), understanding the lag geometrically and algebraically (§2), designing one and discovering the slow tail (§3), understanding where the tail comes from and where it matters (§4), making the same improvement with PI (§5), comparing the two (§6), and then surveying three extensions that use the same “change the pattern” idea, each on FPE’s own example (§§7–9).

1. Specify the accuracy improvement

The baseline controller is Dc​=1. Its closed-loop poles are −0.5±0.8660j, with ζ=0.5, 16.30% overshoot, and a 1% settling time of about 8.8 s. Its velocity constant is

Kv​=s→0lim​sDc​G=1 s−1.

For a ramp of slope v, ess​=v/Kv​. We seek a factor-five reduction for unit slope: from 1 to at most 0.2, so Kv​≥5. We would like to keep the early transient reasonably close to the baseline. This is a tracking specification plus a transient preference; we will measure the change rather than assume it is zero.

Raising the proportional gain to 5 would meet Kv​=5, but the polynomial s2+s+5 has ζ=1/(25​)=0.224 and about 48.6% overshoot. This is Lecture II’s conflict on the simplest possible plant: transient and accuracy requirements sit at opposite ends of one branch. Instead of sliding along the branch, raise the gain near s=0 while keeping the compensator close to unity near the original pair.

2. The lag compensator: form, geometry, and the general algebra

FPE’s form and the two views of it

FPE §5.4.2 asks for “an expression for Dc​(s) that will yield a significant gain at s=0 to raise Kv​ … but is nearly unity (no effect) at the higher frequency ωn​, where dynamic response is determined.” The result is Eq. (5.72),

Dc​(s)=Ks+ps+z​,z>p>0,

with z and p very small compared with ωn​, yet Dc​(0)=Kz/p equal to 3 to 10, “the value depending on the extent to which the steady-state gain requires boosting.” Two limits say everything about it:

Dc​(0)=Kpz​,Dc​(s)≈Kfor ∣s∣≫z,p.

Thus with K=1, choose z/p=5. Because z>p, the phase of Dc​(jω) is negative, corresponding to phase lag; hence the name.

Why the pair leaves the locus nearly alone

Start with the baseline closed-loop pole A=−0.5+0.866j on the uncompensated locus, where the angle condition holds. Suppose we add a pole at the origin to raise the system type. The new arrow from the origin to A has angle 120∘, which must be subtracted from the loop angle; the angle condition at A fails, and A is no longer on the locus. Now add a zero very close to that pole. Its arrow to A has almost the same angle, which is added, so the two nearly cancel and A is approximately back on the locus. The lengths of the two arrows are also nearly equal, so the magnitude condition, and therefore the gain at A, is nearly unchanged. Nise’s conclusion is Eq. (9.5): with the gain essentially the same, the new velocity constant is the old one multiplied by z/p.

The only way to have both a large ratio z/p and a small angle contribution is to put the pair close to the origin: Nise’s example is a pole at −0.001 and a zero at −0.01, ratio 10, angle contribution negligible. Ogata’s §6–7 rule is to hold the lag’s angle at the dominant pole to less than 5∘. The dominant pair here sits about one unit from the origin, and the new pole and zero will be a few hundredths away from it, so the condition is met comfortably. “Nearly” is the operative word, and §3 measures how nearly.

The general characteristic polynomial, once

For the motor G=1/[s(s+a)] with lag Dc​=K(s+z)/(s+p), the characteristic equation is

1+s(s+a)(s+p)K(s+z)​=0,

P(s)=s(s+a)(s+p)+K(s+z)=s3+(a+p)s2+(ap+K)s+Kz​.

The Routh array for the cubic is

s3s2s1s0​1a+pa+p(a+p)(ap+K)−Kz​Kz​ap+KKz00​,

so with positive a,p,z,K the loop is stable exactly when

(a+p)(ap+K)>Kz.

For a=1, K=1, z=5p this reads (1+p)2>5p, or p2−3p+1>0, which holds for the small p we will use but fails for 0.382<p<2.618. Do not conclude that every positive lag pole-zero choice is stable.

The three transfer functions we will need

With P in hand, the sensitivity, the reference path, and the disturbance path are

S=Ps(s+a)(s+p)​,T=RY​=PK(s+z)​,GS=WY​=Ps+p​.

Notice the zeros. The reference path has the controller zero at −z in its numerator; the disturbance path has the controller pole at −p instead. That difference is the whole of §4.

The two steady-state numbers follow from the final-value theorem, which applies once stability is established. For a unit ramp, E=SR with R=1/s2:

ess,ramp​=s→0lim​sSs21​=s→0lim​P(s+a)(s+p)​=Kzap​=Kv​1​,Kv​=apKz​.

For a unit step disturbance at the plant input,

yss​=s→0lim​s(GS)s1​=Kzp​=Dc​(0)1​.

So the lag does what we want at s=0: with K=1 and z/p=5 on the normalized motor, Kv​=5 exactly, and a constant input disturbance is attenuated by the same factor. What it does to the transient is a question the algebra answers only after we pick numbers.

3. Design the lag: first candidate, the tail, and the revision

First candidate and its angle check

Take z=0.05, p=0.01, K=1, which is FPE’s own lag pair from §5.4.2. At the old pole sd​=−0.5+0.8660j the lag’s angle contribution is

arg(sd​+0.05)−arg(sd​+0.01)=atan2(0.8660,−0.45)−atan2(0.8660,−0.49)=117.457∘−119.501∘≈−2.04∘.

This is small but not zero. The old pair is no longer exactly on the new locus. (If the gain were retuned to put a pole back on a chosen damping ray, the exact error-constant improvement would be (Knew​/Kold​)(z/p), not simply z/p. We keep K=1.)

Substituting a=1, p=0.01, K=1, z=0.05 into §2’s polynomial,

P=s3+1.01s2+1.01s+0.05.

Its roots are −0.05208 and −0.47896±0.85482j. Stability follows independently from the Routh condition: all coefficients are positive and 1.01⋅1.01>0.05. The error constant is exactly Kv​=5, hence the unit-ramp error is exactly 0.2.

The reference step has about 21.56% overshoot and 28.32 s settling time (1%), compared with 16.30% and about 8.8 s for the baseline. A small angular change was not enough to preserve the transient.

What went wrong? Look at the roots. The complex pair moved only slightly. That small shift explains some extra overshoot, but not a tripled settling time. The culprit is the third root at −0.052, which did not exist before. The lag pole and zero near the origin have captured one closed-loop pole between them, and it decays with a time constant of about 19 s. Section 4 explains why its effect on the reference step is small but not negligible, and why its effect elsewhere is larger.

Revised lag design

Move both locations five times closer to the origin, retaining the ratio:

Dc​(s)=s+0.002s+0.01​​.

The characteristic polynomial is

P=s3+1.002s2+1.002s+0.01​.

The poles are approximately −0.010081 and −0.49596±0.86373j. Routh gives 1.0022>0.01, so the loop is stable. Kv​ remains exactly 5. The angle perturbation at the original pair is now 119.501∘−119.901∘≈−0.40∘.

Moving the pair inward makes the captured pole even slower, with a time constant near 99 s. It also moves that pole closer to the zero at −z, which shrinks its share of the reference step response. The trade is a longer but much smaller tail, and for the reference step the smaller amplitude wins: the step now overshoots 17.38% and enters its 1% band for the last time at about 12.0 s. This is faster than the first candidate’s 28.32 s but slower than the baseline’s roughly 8.8 s. The accuracy improvement still costs some settling time, even though the early response remains close to baseline.

This design meets the specified ramp accuracy and keeps the early transient close to baseline. If there were a strict 16.3% overshoot limit, further redesign would be needed.

4. Why the small pole still matters

Here is the mechanism behind the tail, and why its size depends on which input you look at.

Where the slow pole comes from

The close-up loci in the figure below show how the captured pole arises. At very small gain the two near-origin poles, at 0 and −p, leave the real axis, circle around the lag zero, and land again just to its left. One of them then travels right toward the zero at −z and is captured there; the other heads left to meet the motor pole from −1 and form the familiar dominant pair. By K=1 the captured pole has nearly reached the zero. FPE’s Fig. 5.28 shows the same circular locus near the origin for the lead–lag design of §7.

The closeups show only the branches near the origin. At K=1, the complex pair lies outside each window; the marked square is the slow real root.

A one-line estimate locates that root without solving the cubic. For a small real root q, the s3 and s2 terms of P are negligible, so P(q)≈(ap+K)q+Kz=0 and

q≈−ap+KKz​.

For the revised design this gives −0.00998, close to the exact −0.010081, and it sits just to the left of the zero at −z=−0.01, as the locus says it must.

Residues in the two paths

For a simple closed-loop pole q, the coefficient of eqt in the unit-step response of a path N(s)/P(s) is N(q)/[qP′(q)]. For the reference and disturbance paths of §2,

rR​=qP′(q)K(q+z)​,rW​=qP′(q)q+p​,P′(s)=3s2+2(a+p)s+(ap+K).

The two residues share a denominator and differ only in the numerator factor: q+z for the reference, q+p for the disturbance. The slow root lies almost on top of −z, so q+z is tiny and rR​ is small. It lies nowhere near −p on that scale, so rW​ is not small. Numerically:

For the first candidate, the slow term in the unit reference step is 0.04369e−0.05208t, which by itself stays above 1% for ln(4.369)/0.05208≈28.3 s; that is the settling time we measured. In the revised design the slow residue is already below 1%, so the reference step can enter its band at 12 s even though the mode’s time constant is 99 s. In the disturbance path the same mode appears at nearly full strength in every design, and no choice of z and p hides it there.

What the disturbance response looks like

For a unit step disturbance at the plant input, §2 gave

yss​=Dc​(0)1​=0.2,ess​=−0.2,

and the approach to that value is governed by the slow mode with residue rW​≈0.8: the motor position is pushed by the disturbance and the lag’s extra low-frequency gain pulls it back on a 99 s time scale. The ramp-tracking error approaches its final value of 0.2 on the same time scale, for the same reason. Simulate long enough to see that approach; a short reference-step plot is insufficient. FPE says the same about its lead–lag design: the slow transient “will be much more evident” in the response to a disturbance torque.

5. PI: push the pole to the origin

A finite DC gain leaves a finite ramp error. To eliminate it, make the DC gain infinite: move the lag pole to the origin and increase the loop type from 1 to 2. Nise §9.2 makes this distinction explicit: lag improves an error constant; PI adds an integrator. Nise calls PI an “ideal integral compensator” and notes that it needs an active implementation, whereas a lag can be a passive network.

Use

Dc​(s)=Kss+z​=K+sKz​,kP​=K,kI​=Kz.

Choose K=1, z=0.01 to keep the added zero near the added pole at the origin:

Dc​(s)=1+s0.01​​,P=s3+s2+s+0.01​,

which is §2’s polynomial with p=0.

Stability before final values

For general K,z>0 and motor pole a, P=s3+as2+Ks+Kz and

s3s2s1s0​1aaK(a−z)​Kz​KKz00​.

Stability requires K>0 and 0<z<a. With the motor pole normalized to a=1 s−1 this is 0<z<1; for a motor denominator s(s+a) the bound is z<a, not a universal numerical bound of one.

Two boundary cases teach what the condition means. At z=a,

P=s3+as2+Ks+Ka=(s+a)(s2+K),

so the pair is undamped on the imaginary axis. The PI zero cancels the motor pole in the loop transfer function, and what remains is a double integrator under proportional control, which Lecture II showed has no damping at any gain. A cancellation does not rescue stability. The other case is no zero at all: pure integral control Dc​=K/s gives P=s3+as2+K, whose Routh s1 entry is −K/a<0 for every K>0. Its locus has a double pole at the origin departing at ±90∘ and three branches to infinity at ±60∘ and 180∘; two go straight into the right half-plane. Pure integral control of this motor is unstable at every gain.

Our selected poles are approximately −0.010101 and −0.49495±0.86315j, all stable. The angle perturbation at the old pair is 119.501∘−120∘≈−0.50∘.

Tracking and disturbance checks

There are two uncanceled loop poles at the origin:

L(s)=s2(s+1)K(s+z)​,S=Ps2(s+a)​,GS=Ps​.

With stability established, the final-value theorem applies. Using R=1/s, 1/s2, and 1/s3 for the unit step, ramp, and t2/2 parabola,

ess,step​=s→0lim​Ps2(s+a)​=0,ess,ramp​=s→0lim​Ps(s+a)​=0,ess,parabola​=s→0lim​Ps+a​=Kza​=Ka​1​,

with Ka​=lims→0​s2L=Kz=0.01 s−2. A unit parabolic reference therefore leaves ess​=100: zero ramp error is not perfect tracking of every trajectory. For the plant-input step disturbance, yss​=lims→0​s/P=0.

The disturbance is eventually rejected, but the slow mode still takes about 100 s per time constant, and §4’s table shows its disturbance residue is about one. The unit reference step overshoots 17.66% and has about 12.76 s 1% settling time. As with lag, the reference transient can look fast while disturbance rejection is slow.

Taking p→0 at fixed z,K connects lag to PI, but the long-time behavior changes: the finite DC gain becomes an integrator. Setting both p=z=0 cancels the intended factor and leaves proportional control; that does not increase the type.

FPE’s design procedure for lag compensation (8th ed., end of §5.4.2), in outline:

  1. Determine the gain amplification the lag must contribute at low frequency to achieve the desired Kp​, Kv​, or Ka​.
  2. Select p roughly a factor of 100 to 200 smaller than the dominant natural frequency.
  3. Select z so that z/p equals the required amplification.
  4. Examine the resulting locus to verify that the dominant roots are still satisfactory; if not, adjust the lead compensation.
  5. Verify every time-domain specification against the unit-step response. If the slow root is too slow, increase z and p while keeping z/p constant, understanding that the closer they come to the dominant roots, the more they affect them.

Our revised design has p=0.002, which is 500 times smaller than the baseline ωn​=1: more conservative than FPE’s step 2, for the reasons discussed at the end of §3.

7. Extension: lag–lead, using FPE’s design

The remaining sections are variations on one theme: when the locus does not go where you need it, change the pattern of poles and zeros that generates it. Three such patterns are common enough to have names, and FPE works all three on the same motor.

Lead and lag act in different frequency ranges, so they can be combined. A lead section shapes the dominant branches; a lag section then raises the low-frequency gain without disturbing them much. FPE §5.4.2 starts from the revised lead of Example 5.11:

Dlead​=91s+13s+2​,Kv​=s→0lim​sDlead​G=1391⋅2​=14.

To obtain Kv​=70, a factor-five reduction of the velocity error, the book adds the lag (s+0.05)/(s+0.01), with z/p=5:

Dc​=91(s+13)(s+0.01)(s+2)(s+0.05)​,Kv​=13⋅0.0191⋅2⋅0.05​=70.

The loop numerator and denominator are

91(s+2)(s+0.05)=91s2+186.55s+9.1,s(s+1)(s+13)(s+0.01)=s4+14.01s3+13.14s2+0.13s,

and their sum is the characteristic polynomial

P=s4+14.01s3+104.14s2+186.68s+9.1.

For s4+As3+Bs2+Cs+D the Routh first column is 1, A, b1​=(AB−C)/A, c1​=(b1​C−AD)/b1​, D. Here b1​≈90.815 and c1​≈185.276, both positive, so the loop is stable. The roots are approximately −0.050140, −2.3967, and −5.7816±6.5038j. FPE reports the dominant roots at −5.8±6.5j, essentially where the lead alone put them, and a closed-loop root “very near the lag-compensation zero at −0.05.” The computed reference step has about 17.47% overshoot and 0.190 s rise time, preserving the original 20% and 0.3 s requirements, and the unit-ramp error is 1/70.

The two sections are designed for different purposes, but they interact. Verify the combined system after adding the second section. “Lead first, lag second” is a practical sequence, not a theorem that the first design remains unchanged. And §4’s warning applies in full: the slow root’s reference residue is small because of the zero at −0.05, and its disturbance residue is not.

8. Extension: a notch for a flexible mode, using FPE’s design

FPE’s scenario

The second pattern targets a resonance. FPE §5.4.3 continues the story: the lead–lag design of §7 is built and tested, and “is found to have a substantial oscillation at about 50 rad/s,” because there was an unsuspected noncollocated flexibility. On reexamination the plant is estimated to be FPE’s Eq. (5.74),

G(s)=s(s+1)(s2+s+2500)2500​,

a lightly damped mode at ω=50 rad/s with ζ=0.01 added to the rigid motor. Lecture II’s noncollocated satellite showed what to expect: the departure-angle calculation at such a pole often shows feedback pushing it toward instability. Our own computation on FPE’s numbers confirms the symptom. With the lead–lag loop closed around the flexible plant, the flexible pair moves from the open-loop −0.5±50j to −0.29±49.1j: still stable, but with half the damping it started with, and the step response carries a 50 rad/s ripple that keeps the 1% settling time at 5.5 s. The departure angle at −0.5+50j is −78∘, pointing down and to the right.

FPE names two remedies. Gain stabilization lowers the loop gain at the resonance, for instance with more lag, so that less “control energy” spills into the mode. Phase stabilization adds a zero pair near the resonance to turn the departure angle toward the left half-plane, which is what the collocated zeros did for free in Lecture II. The device for phase stabilization is the notch, FPE’s Eq. (5.75),

Dnotch​(s)=(s+ω0​)2s2+2ζω0​s+ω02​​,

a lightly damped zero pair at ω0​ backed by a critically damped pole pair at the same radius, so that Dnotch​(0)=1 and Kv​ is unchanged.

Above or below the resonance?

“A necessary design decision is whether to place the notch frequency above or below that of the natural resonance.” FPE settles it with the angle of departure: the notch must sit above the resonance for the branch to leave toward the left half-plane. The reason is geometric. A zero pair just above the flexible pole contributes an arrow pointing nearly straight down, about −90∘ each from the upper zero and little from the lower one, which rotates the departure direction by roughly a quarter turn; a pair just below contributes arrows pointing nearly straight up, which rotates it the other way. FPE’s choice is Eq. (5.76),

Dnotch​(s)=(s+60)2s2+0.8s+3600​,

with zeros at −0.4±60.0j. With the notch in the loop the departure angle at the flexible pole becomes −158∘, pointing down and to the left, and the closed-loop flexible pair moves to −0.65±49.9j: more damped than the open-loop mode, not less. For comparison, a notch of the same shape placed at 40 rad/s gives a departure angle of about 0∘ and leaves the flexible pair at −0.31±50.0j, essentially unhelped. The direction, not just the depth, of the notch is what matters.

What the notch costs

The notch is not free either. Its pole pair at −60 and its phase lag below ω0​ reach down into the servo band. With the notch, the dominant pair moves from −5.99±6.53j to −4.02±7.46j, the damping ratio falls from 0.68 to 0.47, and the computed step overshoot rises from 19.4% to 30.8%, while the 10–90% rise time stays at 0.16 s and the 1% settling time falls from 5.5 s to 1.56 s. That is FPE’s own reading of its Fig. 5.30: “the notch damps the oscillations well but degrades the overshoot somewhat. The rise time specification was not affected. To rectify the increased overshoot and strictly meet all the specifications, further iteration should be carried out.” The lower-right panel of the figure in §9 shows both responses.

How much a detuned notch helps

A notch is only as good as its tuning. To see how fast its attenuation falls off, take a general notch with separately chosen zero and pole damping,

DN​(s)=s2+2ζp​ωf​s+ωf2​s2+2ζz​ωf​s+ωf2​​,0<ζz​<ζp​,

of which FPE’s form is the case ζp​=1. For a stable filter, substituting s=jω gives the gain applied to a sinusoid at frequency ω; this is the frequency-response idea already introduced with harmonic forcing. Writing the numerator and denominator as real plus imaginary parts,

DN​(jω)=(ωf2​−ω2)+j2ζp​ωf​ω(ωf2​−ω2)+j2ζz​ωf​ω​,∣DN​(jω)∣2=(ωf2​−ω2)2+(2ζp​ωf​ω)2(ωf2​−ω2)2+(2ζz​ωf​ω)2​.

At ω=ωf​ the real parts vanish and ∣DN​(jωf​)∣=ζz​/ζp​; at ω=0 and ω→∞ the gain is 1. For a course illustration take ωf​=10 rad/s, ζz​=0.03, ζp​=0.30:

DN​(s)=s2+6s+100s2+0.6s+100​,

with poles at −3±j91​ and zeros at −0.3±j99.91​. It transmits 10% of a 10 rad/s sinusoid’s amplitude. At 8 rad/s the ratio is (362+4.82)/(362+482)​=0.605, and at 12 rad/s it is (442+7.22)/(442+722)​=0.528. A 20% shift in the resonance frequency costs most of the attenuation.

This is FPE’s closing warning: phase stabilization “depends on maintaining the correct phase at the frequency of the resonance. If that frequency is subject to significant change, which is common in many cases, then the notch needs to be removed far enough from the nominal frequency in order to work for all cases,” at the price of interfering with the rest of the dynamics. “As a general rule, gain stabilization is substantially more robust to plant changes than is phase stabilization.” Include the flexible dynamics, inspect the complete locus and departure directions, and check plausible frequency variation. Do not rely on exact cancellation of an uncertain lightly damped plant pole, and never use cancellation of an unstable pole as a stability argument.

9. Extension: feedback compensation and successive loop closure

The third pattern changes not the controller’s transfer function but where the measurement enters. Lecture III noted that a derivative acting on the measured output, rather than on the error, gives the same poles without the reference zero, and FPE’s §5.4 introduction said the same of compensation placed in the feedback path. Here is that arrangement, now seen as a root-locus design, on FPE’s Example 5.15 (7th ed. Fig. 5.40, 8th ed. Fig. 5.44).

The structure

A velocity sensor, a tachometer in FPE’s example, provides damping inside a position loop. Let

u=K(r−y)−kv​y˙​,G=s(s+1)1​.

Substituting into Y=GU,

Y=G[KR−(K+kv​s)Y]⟹[s(s+1)+kv​s+K]Y=KR,

RY​=s2+(1+kv​)s+KK​​.

Compare the forward-path PD u=(K+kv​s)(r−y): the denominator is identical, but the numerator becomes K+kv​s. Only the zeros from R to Y change. The disturbance and noise responses, which depend on the loop, do not.

FPE’s two-parameter locus

FPE’s characteristic equation, s2+s+KA​+KT​s=0 in the book’s notation (KA​=K, KT​=kv​), has two parameters, and a root locus handles one at a time. The book’s procedure is to fix KA​=4 and draw the locus against KT​,

1+KT​s2+s+4s​=0,

with a zero at the origin and poles at −0.5±1.94j: the tachometer gain drags the pair around toward the real axis, adding damping. Choosing KT​=1 gives s2+2s+4, roots −1±j3​, ζ=0.5. Then re-form the equation with KA​=4+K1​ and draw the locus against K1​, whose starting poles are the roots just found. One can “see-saw” between the parameters in this way. FPE calls the general technique successive loop closure (§5.6.2): close an inner loop around part of the dynamics, then design a simpler outer loop around the result.

Our numbers

Choose K=8, kv​=3. The poles are again −2±2j, but Y/R=8/(s2+4s+8) has no PD reference zero, and the unit-step response is

y(t)=1−e−2t(cos2t+sin2t),

with its first peak at tp​=π/2 s and overshoot e−π=4.321%: the standard pair’s number at last, because the numerator is the standard pair’s numerator. The initial command is u(0+)=K=8 for a reference step, finite because the reference is not differentiated. Measurement noise still passes through the derivative; filter it in a realizable implementation, and include the actual sensor and filter dynamics before treating this ideal derivative feedback as a realizable design.

Equivalently, first close the velocity loop,

Ginner​=1+kv​sGG​=s(s+1+kv​)1​,

then use K in the outer position loop: the inner loop has moved the motor pole from −1 to −(1+kv​)=−4, and proportional control of 1/[s(s+4)] with K=8 gives s2+4s+8 directly.

What to take away

  • A lag compensator is a pole-zero pair close together near the origin. Seen from the dominant poles the pair nearly cancels; seen from s=0 its ratio z/p multiplies the error constant.
  • The motor’s lag loop has P=s3+(a+p)s2+(ap+K)s+Kz, is stable when (a+p)(ap+K)>Kz, and has ramp error ap/(Kz) and disturbance offset p/(Kz). Every candidate is a substitution into these.
  • The pair captures one closed-loop pole near the lag zero, at about −Kz/(ap+K). Its residue in the reference step is small because Y/R has the zero at −z; the same mode appears at nearly full strength in the disturbance response, whose zero is at −p, and in the approach to the final ramp error. Simulate long enough to see it.
  • PI is lag with the pole at the origin. The DC gain becomes infinite and the type goes up by one, so ramp error and plant-input disturbance offset both vanish. The zero is what keeps the loop stable: 0<z<a on this motor, and pure integral control is unstable at every gain.
  • Lag–lead, notch, and rate feedback are the same idea in three more forms: change the pattern of poles and zeros, or where the measurement enters, and re-check the complete locus and response. The notch is Rule 4 used as a design tool, and it must sit on the correct side of the resonance.

The root-locus toolkit, in one place

Across the four lectures the method reduced to a few moves. A gain slides every closed-loop pole along fixed branches (I, II). A left-half-plane zero pulls the branches toward it, which is how PD and lead improve the transient (III). A pole pushes them away, which is why lead’s extra pole and every unmodeled lag cost stability margin (I, III). A near-origin pole-zero pair raises the low-frequency gain while leaving the branches almost alone, which is how lag and PI improve accuracy (IV). A zero pair near a lightly damped mode turns its departure angle toward safety (IV). Every one of these moves is checked the same way: find all the poles at the chosen gain, form the complete transfer function for the input that matters, and look at the response. Part V develops the same designs from the frequency response, where the cost of each move appears as phase.
