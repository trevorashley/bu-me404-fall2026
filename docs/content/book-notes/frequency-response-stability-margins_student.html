Stability Margins

Franklin, Powell, and Emami-Naeini, Feedback Control of Dynamic Systems, 8th ed., §6.4, Examples 6.12–6.13. The corresponding 7th-edition section is on pp. 348–357. Ogata, Modern Control Engineering, 5th ed., §7-7, gives a second treatment of gain and phase margins. Åström and Murray, Feedback Systems, 2nd ed., §10.3, develop the shortest-distance interpretation.

Prerequisites. Review Bode plots, the imaginary-axis stability boundary, and the Nyquist criterion. The time-domain specifications note introduces the standard second-order step response.

Objectives. Read gain and phase margins at their respective crossovers, select a gain to meet a phase-margin target, and relate phase margin to damping for a specified model. Use vector distance and a pole count when several crossings or a gain-reduction boundary complicate the interpretation.

We use real, continuous-time systems with negative feedback. L denotes the full loop transfer function, including the controller, plant, and sensor. The examples use a unity sensor. When the controller is a scalar K and the plant is G, L=KG and T=L/(1+L) is the reference-to-output transfer function. Frequencies are in rad/s and phase angles are in degrees.

1. How much gain or phase change brings the loop to the boundary?

Start with a stable closed loop. Its characteristic equation is 1+L(s)=0. To place a closed-loop pole at jω, a change in the loop must bring the complex point L(jω) to −1. Two separate experiments give the classical margins.

Change gain alone. A positive multiplier g scales the radius of a point without changing its angle. It can bring L(jω) to −1 only if that point already lies on the negative real axis. Its frequency ωpc​ is a phase crossover, where the phase is −180∘ modulo 360∘. There L=−∣L∣, so solving gL=−1 gives the gain margin and its decibel equivalent,

GM=∣L(jωpc​)∣1​,GMdB​=20log10​GM.

For example, GM=2 means the gain can reach the boundary by doubling. In the usual stable single-crossover case, if there is no finite phase crossover the gain margin is infinite. A phase change or an unmodeled resonance can still bring the loop to the boundary.

Change phase alone. A pure lag of δ rotates a point clockwise through δ and preserves its radius. To reach −1 by rotation, start where ∣L(jωc​)∣=1. This is a gain crossover, or a 0 dB crossing. On the phase branch near −180∘, solve argL(jωc​)−δ=−180∘. The phase margin is

PM=180∘+argL(jωc​).

A positive value gives the additional lag required to reach the boundary. The figure uses the unit-gain plant developed in Section 2. Trace the radial move on the negative real axis, then the angular move along the unit circle.

At 1 rad/s the loop is −0.5, giving GM=2 or 6.02 dB. At 0.682 rad/s the magnitude is already one, and a 21.39∘ clockwise rotation reaches −1. On a Bode plot these two changes are vertical differences read at different frequencies.

MATLAB convention. In MATLAB’s four-output margin calculation, Wcg is the frequency associated with gain margin (phase crossover). Wcp is associated with phase margin (gain crossover).

A stability claim also needs the count from the Nyquist note: Z=N+P, where P counts open-loop right-half-plane (RHP) poles and N counts net clockwise turns of the complete loop image around −1. Asymptotic stability requires Z=0, with no boundary or hidden nondecaying modes. The last two examples show why it helps to establish nominal stability before interpreting a margin.

2. Select gain for a specified phase margin

For the recurring loop L=K/[s(s+1)2], take K>0. The integrator contributes a factor ω to the denominator magnitude and 90∘ to its angle. Each copy of 1+jω contributes 1+ω2​ and tan−1ω. Thus

∣L(jω)∣=ω(1+ω2)K​,ϕ(ω)=−90∘−2tan−1ω.

At ϕ=−180∘, tan−1ω=45∘, hence ωpc​=1 and GM=2/K. At unity magnitude the equation is ωc3​+ωc​=K. Its left side increases strictly with ωc​, so each positive gain has exactly one gain crossover. Substitution gives PM=90∘−2tan−1ωc​.

3. When phase margin predicts damping and overshoot

FPE makes this connection for a no-zero second-order closed loop with ωn​>0, ζ>0:

L=s(s+2ζωn​)ωn2​​,T=s2+2ζωn​s+ωn2​ωn2​​.

Let x=ωc​/ωn​. Taking magnitudes at crossover and squaring gives

1=xx2+4ζ2​1​,(x2)2+4ζ2x2−1=0,x2=1+4ζ4​−2ζ2.

Only the positive solution for x2 is physical. The loop angle is −90∘−tan−1(x/(2ζ)). The positive complementary angle therefore gives

PM=tan−1(x2ζ​)=tan−1​1+4ζ4​−2ζ2​2ζ​​.

The exact curve is approximately linear through about 60∘. FPE uses the straight-line rule only as a rough estimate below about 70∘. The open-loop phase here approaches −180∘ only at infinite frequency, so this family has infinite GM even when its damping is very small.

Step peak. For 0<ζ<1 write ωd​=ωn​1−ζ2​. With zero initial conditions the unit-step response and its derivative are

y(t)=1−e−ζωn​t(cosωd​t+ωd​ζωn​​sinωd​t),y˙​(t)=ωd​ωn2​​e−ζωn​tsinωd​t.

The derivative first changes from positive to negative at tp​=π/ωd​. Since y(∞)=1, the fractional overshoot is

Mp​=y(tp​)−1=e−πζ/1−ζ2​(0<ζ<1).

For ζ≥1 the step rises monotonically, so Mp​=0.

Frequency-response peak. Set r=ω/ωn​. Then ∣T(jω)∣2=1/D(r) with D=(1−r2)2+4ζ2r2. The maximum magnitude occurs at the minimum of D. Differentiating gives D′=4r(r2+2ζ2−1). A positive-frequency minimum exists only for 0<ζ<1/2​. Substituting r2=1−2ζ2 gives

Mr​=2ζ1−ζ2​1​(0<ζ<1/2​).

For ζ≥1/2​, D increases for r>0 and the peak is the DC magnitude, Mr​=1. The two plots use the same phase-margin axis to compare the step peak with the sinusoidal peak.

The sinusoidal peak reaches its DC floor at a lower damping ratio than the one that eliminates step overshoot. Mp​ measures excess above the final step value, while Mr​ is the largest sinusoidal output-to-input amplitude ratio.

Watch out. A closed-loop zero can change a step peak even with the same poles. For a higher-order design, check its poles and step response directly before using a phase-margin reading to predict overshoot.

4. Measure the closest approach to the critical point

GM follows a radial change. PM follows a rotation. A curve can pass close to −1 between those two crossings. At any frequency the displacement from −1 to L has real component 1+ℜL and imaginary component ℑL. Its shortest length over the entire frequency response is the vector margin,

dv​=ωmin​∣1+L(jω)∣,∣1+L∣=(1+ℜL)2+(ℑL)2​.

For real coefficients the negative-frequency branch is the complex conjugate of the positive branch, so both give the same distances. Include the limiting endpoints at zero and infinity. The figure returns to the stable K=1 cubic. Its shortest distance is about 0.2970 at 0.7666 rad/s.

The closest point falls between ωc​=0.6823 and ωpc​=1 rad/s. A positive distance tells how closely the curve approaches the critical point. Curves with different winding counts can have the same positive distance, so interpreting this as a reserve of stability requires an independent nominal stability check.

5. Example 6.12 — A gain reduction can destabilize the loop

For L=K(s+10)2/s3, consider positive gain. Clearing the denominator of 1+L gives

q=s3+K(s+10)2=s3+Ks2+20Ks+100K.

The Routh first column is 1,K,(K⋅20K−100K)/K,100K, or 1,K,20(K−5),100K. For K>5 all entries are positive. For 0<K<5 two sign changes give two RHP poles. At K=5 the polynomial factors as (s+5)(s2+100), placing two poles at ±j10.

Take the nominal gain K=7. At 10 rad/s the unscaled plant evaluates to G(j10)=−1/5, so L(j10)=−7/5. Multiplying by g=5/7 brings this point to −1. The gain margin is 0.714286, or −2.923 dB. This means a 28.6% reduction takes the nominal gain down from 7 to the stability boundary at 5.

The integrators contribute −270∘ and the two zeros contribute 2tan−1(ω/10). Taking magnitudes and setting the result to unity gives

∣L∣=ω37(ω2+100)​,ωc3​−7ωc2​−700=0.

Solving gives ωc​=11.923603 rad/s and PM=180∘−270∘+2tan−1(ωc​/10)=10.0287∘. A gain reduction reaches −1 at 10 rad/s. Extra lag reaches it at 11.923603 rad/s. Both changes can produce an imaginary-axis pole pair.

The open-loop poles at zero are excluded from the clockwise RHP contour by a rightward indent s=ϵejθ, with θ increasing from −π/2 to +π/2. Near zero, L∼100K/s3, so its angle changes by −3π radians, a 540∘ clockwise sweep. Include that arc when counting. The net count at K=7 is N=0 and P=0, giving Z=0, consistent with Routh. Compare the complete finite contour with the detail at −1 below.

The square pole markers sit on the boundary for the conjugate pair at K=5. At K=7 all three circles are in the left half-plane. The middle panel uses a finite indent so its image fits on the page. Shrinking the indent moves its arc outward while preserving the count.

Design implication. In this system, turning the gain below 5 to tame an oscillation introduces two growing modes. The Routh count identifies which direction of gain change preserves stability.

6. Example 6.13 — Resolve several gain crossovers

Keep FPE’s full loop model,

L(s)=s2(s2+2s+82)(s2+2s+101)85(s+1)(s2+2s+43.25)​.

Completing the squares gives a zero pair at −1±6.5j and pole pairs at −1±9j and −1±10j, in addition to the real zero and the double integrator. The nearby pole pairs lift the magnitude back through unity at high frequency.

Set x=ω2. Each quadratic s2+2s+c has squared magnitude (c−x)2+4x on the imaginary axis. Equating the squared numerator and denominator magnitudes yields

7225(x+1)[(43.25−x)2+4x]=x2[(82−x)2+4x][(101−x)2+4x].

Solving this polynomial for all positive real roots gives x=0.553013, 89.323654, 96.802497. Take their square roots and evaluate the loop phase at each frequency. The table and its Bode plots show the three distinct readings.

For a phase crossover we need a negative real loop value. If L=B/A, then L=BA/∣A∣2. On the nonzero frequency axis the denominator is positive. Setting the imaginary part of BA to zero and removing its nonzero scale and ω3 factors gives

4x3−881x2+61891x−1435724=0.

The sole positive real root is x=106.979882. At ωpc​=10.343108 rad/s the loop evaluates to about −0.792108, giving GM=1.262453 or 2.0243 dB. A separate minimum-distance search gives dv​=0.187206 at 10.272733 rad/s. The closest approach occurs slightly before the phase crossover.

Finally compute 4(A+B) to clear the fractional coefficient. The characteristic polynomial is

q=4s6+16s5+748s4+1804s3+34148s2+15385s+14705.

Its roots are −0.139334±10.264271j, −1.638555±8.718046j, and −0.222111±0.627713j. All three pairs decay. In the Nyquist check, the indent excludes the double pole at zero and maps through a full clockwise turn because L behaves as a positive constant times 1/s2. The complete contour gives N=0 and P=0, hence Z=0. With nominal stability established, examine the clearance in the enlarged frequency-response view below.

The square lies on the negative real axis and determines the gain margin. The short segment ends off that axis and determines the vector margin. The small clearance near the high-frequency resonances would be easy to overlook if only the first gain crossover were recorded.

Watch out. A single phase margin can conceal additional unit-magnitude crossings. Enumerate the crossings, then use the complete Nyquist count or the closed-loop poles to establish stability.

Takeaway. Identify the perturbation before interpreting a margin. A gain factor, a phase lag, and a distance in the complex plane answer different questions. The nominal pole count tells which side of each boundary the loop occupies.
