BU ENG ME404: Dynamics and Control of Mechanical Systems, Fall 2026

Frequency Response

Student notes — FPE 8th ed., Section 6.1 (before §6.1.1)

A sinusoidal test asks a concrete question: if a motor, joint, or controller is driven repeatedly at one frequency, how large is its settled output, and how far does it lead or lag? Sweeping that frequency reveals which motions are followed, amplified, or attenuated. These measurements also support feedback design when an accurate plant model is difficult to obtain.

Sources: Franklin, Powell, and Emami-Naeini, Feedback Control of Dynamic Systems, 8th ed., §6.1, checked against the 7th ed., printed pp. 309–317. Example numbers 6.1–6.2 belong to FPE. The opening first-order illustration and second-order family also follow FPE; the figures here are newly computed from those models. Ogata, Modern Control Engineering, 5th ed., §7-1, pp. 399–401, independently supports the sinusoidal-response derivation and repeated-pole argument. Added explanations and computational checks are course material.

Prerequisites: Laplace transforms, partial fractions, stable poles, and second-order dynamics. Optional refreshers: convolution and impulse response, signals and Fourier series, and time-domain specifications. The needed sinusoidal and complex-number facts are supplied below; no Fourier transform or complex integration is required.

Learning objectives

After studying these notes, you should be able to:

  1. Compute a sinusoidal steady response and separate it from the transient.
  2. Interpret magnitude and phase as measurable amplitude ratio and relative timing.
  3. Obtain a frequency response by sweeping settled sinusoidal experiments and apply superposition.
  4. Connect second-order damping to resonance, step overshoot, and half-power bandwidth.

Notation and conventions

Use j2=−1, frequency ω in rad/s, and time t in seconds. Phase plots use degrees; angles in analytic exponentials, trigonometric arguments, and time-shift formulas use radians. Convert a numerical phase in degrees by multiplying by π/180 before adding it to ωt. M is a magnitude ratio and ϕ is the output phase relative to the input. Mr​ is a frequency-response peak; Mp​ is fractional step overshoot.

G denotes a plant, Dc​ a controller, and H a sensor. All feedback is negative. The full loop is L=Dc​GH, including every gain; if a gain K is separated from the remaining controller, write L=KDc​GH and count K once. We introduce the reference response T(s) in §5. The scalar time constant T in Example 6.2 is a different quantity.

1. From a switched-on sinusoid to frequency response

1.1 Assumptions and a small complex-number toolkit

Assume a continuous-time LTI system, zero initial conditions, and a proper real-rational transfer function G(s). Real-rational means a ratio of polynomials with real coefficients; proper means the numerator degree does not exceed the denominator degree. All natural modes must lie strictly in the left half plane (LHP), and there must be no pole at the excitation frequencies ±jω0​, where ω0​>0. The natural-mode condition includes any internal modes hidden by a pole-zero cancellation. Properness makes the partial-fraction representation below complete without a polynomial part that could represent switch-on impulses. The capacitor in §2 is treated separately.

A complex number z=a+jb has conjugate z∗=a−jb. Conjugation changes the sign of its imaginary part. Euler’s identity,

ejθ=cosθ+jsinθ,

packages a sinusoid into a rotating complex number; only its real or imaginary part is a physical signal. Its magnitude and angle give

z=M(cosϕ+jsinϕ)=Mejϕ,M=a2+b2​,ϕ=atan2(b,a).

atan2 selects the quadrant from both signs; tan−1(b/a) alone can select the wrong quadrant. At z=0, phase is undefined. For real-coefficient G, G(−jω)=G(jω)∗.

1.2 Natural terms and the forced sinusoid

Switch on u(t)=Asin(ω0​t) at t=0, with A>0. The Laplace transform and zero-state output are

U(s)=s2+ω02​Aω0​​,Y(s)=(s−jω0​)(s+jω0​)Aω0​G(s)​.

For simple plant poles p1​,…,pn​, partial fractions separate the natural and forced terms:

Y(s)=k=1∑n​s−pk​ck​​+s−jω0​r+​​+s+jω0​r−​​.

The constants ck​ and r±​ are the coefficients of their respective fractions, often called residues. They can be found by multiplying by the corresponding denominator factor and evaluating at that pole. For example,

r+​=(s−jω0​)Y(s)∣s=jω0​​=2jω0​Aω0​G(jω0​)​=2jAG(jω0​)​,

r−​=−2jAG(−jω0​)​=r+∗​.

Since L−1{1/(s−p)}=ept, the time response is

y(t)=natural transient∑ck​epk​t​​+yss​(t)r+​ejω0​t+r−​e−jω0​t​​.

Write z=G(jω0​)ejω0​t. The last two terms equal A(z−z∗)/(2j)=AImz. Therefore

yss​(t)=AIm{G(jω0​)ejω0​t}=A∣G(jω0​)∣sin(ω0​t+ϕ),ϕ=argG(jω0​).​

Every natural term decays because Repk​<0. A repeated pole of multiplicity m contributes tqepk​t, q=0,…,m−1; exponential decay still wins over every finite polynomial. Thus y−yss​→0, although yss​ itself keeps oscillating. A cosine input yields the corresponding cosine output with the same relative phase.

Watch out: Evaluating G(jω) is algebra. Interpreting it as the eventual response requires decaying natural modes; an unstable plant does not generally settle to that sinusoid.

Phase convention correction: For a sine input, retain the sine in the boxed result. Changing to cosine would require a −90∘ phase adjustment. This corrects the sine/cosine mismatch in FPE Eq. (6.4).

2. Example 6.1: capacitor

FPE specifies voltage input v and current output i. For an ideal capacitor,

i=Cdtdv​,V(s)I(s)​=Cs

with zero initial voltage in the transfer-function relation. For v=Asinωt, direct differentiation gives

i=ACωcosωt=ACωsin(ωt+90∘).

Thus, for C,ω>0,

M=∣Cjω∣=Cω,ϕ=+90∘.

The ratio is an admittance, with units A/V; it is not a dimensionless gain. Current leads voltage by one quarter cycle. The transfer function Cs is an improper ideal differentiator, so the proper-system transient argument in §1 does not apply unchanged. Direct differentiation is sufficient here; the switched sine starts at zero voltage and creates no voltage jump impulse.

The normalized signals below show this phase relationship without choosing artificial component values.

Current is already at its positive peak when voltage crosses upward through zero. Both have the same frequency; the actual current amplitude grows in proportion to ω.

Don’t make this mistake: Swapping input and output changes Cs into 1/(Cs) and reverses the phase relationship. Name the two signals before writing the transfer function.

3. Example 6.2: lead compensator

FPE uses

Dc​(s)=K1+αTs1+Ts​,K,T>0,0<α<1.

Here T is a time constant in seconds. Substitute s=jω, set q=ωT, and multiply numerator and denominator by the denominator’s conjugate:

Dc​(jω)=K1+α2q2(1+jq)(1−jαq)​=K1+α2q21+αq2+j(1−α)q​.

Alternatively, each factor 1+jq has magnitude 1+q2​ and phase tan−1q. Dividing complex numbers divides magnitudes and subtracts angles, since M1​ejϕ1​/(M2​ejϕ2​)=(M1​/M2​)ej(ϕ1​−ϕ2​). Therefore

M=K1+(αωT)21+(ωT)2​​,ϕ=tan−1(ωT)−tan−1(αωT).​

As ω→0, M→K and ϕ→0. As ω→∞, M→K/α and both arctangents approach 90∘, so ϕ→0 again. At intermediate positive frequencies, ωT>αωT; because arctangent is increasing, ϕ>0. The compensator adds phase lead while increasing high-frequency gain.

For the source values K=T=1, α=0.1,

Dc​(s)=s/10+1s+1​=10s+10s+1​.

The zero is −1 and the pole is −10, giving corner frequencies 1 and 10 rad/s. Here α=0.1 is dimensionless, while αT=0.1 s is the denominator time constant; the pole is −1/(αT)=−10 s−1. At ω=10​,

M=1.111​​=10​,ϕ=tan−110​−tan−1(1/10​)=54.9032∘.

This is a useful numerical checkpoint. General maximization of the lead phase is reserved for the later lead-design notes.

Use MATLAB to reproduce the source’s log-log magnitude and semilog phase plots. bode returns magnitude as a linear ratio and phase in degrees; squeeze removes singleton array dimensions so each is a plotting vector.

4. Measurement and superposition

To measure a stable plant’s frequency response, apply one sinusoid at a time, wait for transients to decay, and fit or read the settled input and output traces. Then

M(ω)=Ain​Aout​​.

If the output lags by Δt, write y=Aout​sin[ω(t−Δt)]. Expanding the argument identifies

ϕ=−ωΔtradians,ϕ=−ωΔtπ180∘​degrees.

The timing of a periodic signal determines phase only modulo 2π (or 360∘). Select a consistent phase branch as frequency varies; jumping between equivalent angles can make a smooth response appear discontinuous. A positive phase indicates lead, so a lagging output has negative phase with this convention. Sweep frequency without changing input amplitude enough to leave the plant’s linear operating region.

A finite Fourier-series idea is simply to express a periodic input as a sum of sinusoids at integer multiples of a base frequency. If

u(t)=k=1∑N​Ak​sin(kω0​t+θk​),

linearity says that the output is the sum of the responses to each component. After all natural terms decay,

yss​(t)=k=1∑N​Ak​∣G(jkω0​)∣sin(kω0​t+θk​+argG(jkω0​)).

Each harmonic keeps its frequency and receives its own amplitude scaling and phase shift. An input’s DC component, if present, contributes G(0) times that constant. Infinite-series representations require convergence considerations; the finite sum already explains why measuring many frequencies is useful. See the prerequisite signals notes for the fuller Fourier-series development.

Connection: The transfer function used for poles and step responses is the same function evaluated at s=jω. We are changing the question asked of the model, not changing the model.

5. Second-order response, resonance, and bandwidth

5.1 Magnitude, phase, and the resonant peak

FPE next compares the unit-DC-gain second-order family

G(s)=s2+2ζωn​s+ωn2​ωn2​​,ωn​>0,ζ>0.

Let r=ω/ωn​. Since (jω)2=−ω2,

G(jω)=1−r2+j2ζr1​,

M(r)=[(1−r2)2+4ζ2r2]−1/2,ϕ(r)=−atan2(2ζr,1−r2).​

The minus sign comes from taking the reciprocal. atan2 makes phase continue through −90∘ toward −180∘ when r>1, where the real part of the denominator is negative.

To maximize magnitude, minimize its positive squared denominator:

D(r)=1+(4ζ2−2)r2+r4,D′(r)=4r(r2+2ζ2−1).

For 0<ζ<1/2​, the nonzero stationary point is rr​=1−2ζ2​. D′ is negative before this point and positive after it, giving a magnitude maximum. At that point,

D(rr​)=(2ζ2)2+4ζ2(1−2ζ2)=4ζ2(1−ζ2).

Therefore

ωr​=ωn​1−2ζ2​,Mr​=2ζ1−ζ2​1​(0<ζ<1/2​).​

For ζ≥1/2​, D′>0 for every r>0, so magnitude decreases from its maximum Mr​=1 at DC; there is no positive-frequency resonant peak. At r=1, independently of that threshold,

M(ωn​)=2ζ1​,ϕ(ωn​)=−90∘.

Thus 1/(2ζ) approximates Mr​ only when ζ is small, and ωn​ is not generally ωr​. The ratio of the exact peak to that approximation is 1/1−ζ2​; at ζ=0.5 it is about 1.155.

Less damping produces a taller, narrower resonance and a faster phase transition near r=1. Every phase curve passes through −90∘ there. The plotted ζ=0.7 is slightly below the exact threshold 1/2​≈0.707107 and has a very small peak (Mr​≈1.00020); it is not the exact no-resonance boundary.

5.2 Compare the step experiment

For 0<ζ<1, let ωd​=ωn​1−ζ2​. The unit-step transform can be decomposed as

sG(s)​=s1​−(s+ζωn​)2+ωd2​s+2ζωn​​=s1​−(s+ζωn​)2+ωd2​s+ζωn​​−(s+ζωn​)2+ωd2​ζωn​​.

Using the shifted sine and cosine Laplace pairs gives the previously studied response

y(t)=1−e−ζωn​t[cos(ωd​t)+1−ζ2​ζ​sin(ωd​t)].

Its derivative is y˙​=(ωn2​/ωd​)e−ζωn​tsinωd​t, so the first maximum occurs at tp​=π/ωd​. Substituting cosπ=−1 and sinπ=0 gives

Mp​=y(tp​)−1=e−πζ/1−ζ2​.​

Mp​ measures the fractional excess above a step’s final value; Mr​ measures the largest settled sinusoidal amplitude ratio. They are different experiments, linked here through the same parameter ζ. Neither formula is a universal relationship for higher-order systems with zeros. When reporting time metrics, use 10–90% rise time, a 1% settling band, and overshoot relative to the actual final value.

5.3 Which transfer function defines tracking bandwidth?

For FPE’s simplified unity-feedback system, the forward path is KG and the full loop is L=KG. The diagram shows negative feedback explicitly.

From E=R−Y and Y=KGE, obtain Y=KG(R−Y), hence

T(s)=R(s)Y(s)​=1+KG(s)KG(s)​=1+L(s)L(s)​,S(s)=R(s)E(s)​=1+L(s)1​.​

Here T(s) denotes a transfer function, distinct from Example 6.2’s scalar time constant T. In a general loop with sensor H, E=R−HY gives Y/R=Dc​G/(1+Dc​GH); it is not automatically L/(1+L).

For a stable low-pass reference response with nonzero DC gain, define bandwidth as the first descending half-power crossing relative to DC:

∣T(jωBW​)∣=2​∣T(0)∣​.

Below this frequency, amplitude tracking is usually useful, although phase and resonance can still matter. The threshold halves the squared amplitude ratio relative to its DC value, hence the name half-power. It is a convention, not a guarantee of a particular tracking error. FPE uses a threshold near 0.707 times the input because its illustrated T(0) is near one. DC normalization states what to do when the final gain differs from one. For a response without low-pass behavior or without such a crossing, this definition need not give a finite bandwidth.

If the closed-loop T has the second-order form from §5.1, its DC gain is one. Set M2=1/2 and let x=rBW2​:

(1−rBW2​)2+4ζ2rBW2​=2,x2+(4ζ2−2)x−1=0.

The quadratic roots have product −1, so exactly one is positive. Selecting it and then taking the positive square root gives

x=1−2ζ2+4ζ4−4ζ2+2​,

ωBW​=ωn​1−2ζ2+4ζ4−4ζ2+2​​.​

The crossing lies on the descending branch: for a resonant system the curve first rises from 1 and later falls through 1/2​; for larger damping it decreases throughout. At ζ=1/2​, the expression is exactly ωBW​=ωn​. At ζ=0.7, it is 1.01005ωn​, so the source’s equality at 0.7 is a rounded statement.

The figure below illustrates the definitions in FPE Fig. 6.5 with a member of this family, ζ=0.3, ωn​=1 rad/s. It is an illustration, not an additional numbered textbook example.

Its peak is Mr​=1.74714 at ωr​=0.905539 rad/s, while its half-power bandwidth is 1.45369 rad/s. The bandwidth lies beyond the peak. Changing ωn​ stretches the frequency axis without changing these normalized values.

Why this matters: Resonance indicates how strongly a sinusoid can be amplified along the transfer path being studied. Bandwidth describes the frequency range of useful tracking; neither is simply the frequency of the largest peak. For a disturbance, first identify its transfer path to the output.

MATLAB’s default bandwidth(T) uses exactly −3 dB, corresponding to 10−3/20≈0.707946, rather than exactly 1/2​. Use bandwidth(T,-10*log10(2)) for half power, or solve the defining equation with a bracketed fzero. The supplied verifier checks both the analytic formula and direct complex evaluation. For unity feedback, E/R=S=1/(1+L) describes tracking error. An additive disturbance d(t), with transform D(s), injected directly at the plant output satisfies Y=L(R−Y)+D, so its output response is Y/D=S with R=0. If instead d is added at the plant input, then Y=G[Dc​(R−Y)+D] and Y/D=GS. Identify the injection point before interpreting a disturbance frequency response. This is FPE’s closing bridge from tracking to disturbance rejection; both responses can be evaluated along s=jω.

Takeaway and review

For a stable proper real-rational LTI model, G(jω) gives the amplitude ratio and relative phase of the eventual sinusoid. Damping connects frequency and step behavior for the standard second-order family. Always specify the transfer path and normalization when giving a bandwidth.
