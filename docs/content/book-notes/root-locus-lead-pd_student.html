The Root-Locus Design Method III — PD and Lead Compensation

Student lecture notes — FPE 8th ed., Sections 5.4 and 5.4.1

When gain alone cannot reach the desired pole region, add controller dynamics. A PD zero changes the root-locus geometry; a lead compensator keeps that useful effect while limiting the controller’s high-frequency gain.

Prerequisites: II — Gain selection, PID control, and derivative implementation.

Sources: FPE 8th ed., §5.4 introduction, §5.4.1 (Figs. 5.22–5.23, Example 5.11, and the lead design procedure), checked against the 7th ed. (pp. 264–270). Nise 7th ed., §9.3, supplies the explicit PD-to-lead design progression and Fig. 9.24’s geometry; Ogata 5th ed., §6–6, supplies the angle-deficiency construction and the requirement to verify the full response. The exact −2±2j design is a [course example] on FPE’s normalized motor plant. FPE Example 5.11 retains its own parameters and specifications in §7.

Learning objectives

  1. Explain, from the root locus, why a controller zero improves the motor’s transient and what an added controller pole costs.
  2. Determine the angle a controller must contribute at a target pole.
  3. Design a PD zero and gain, then a finite-pole lead compensator.
  4. Verify the complete characteristic polynomial and response.
  5. Distinguish controller gain, DC gain, derivative gain, and high-frequency gain.
  6. Explain the tradeoff between pole placement, tracking, noise, and control effort.

Notation and assumptions

Negative unity feedback; L=Dc​G=KL0​. In Dc​=K(s+z)/(s+p), the locations are −z and −p, with z,p>0. Lead has p>z. These positive parameters differ from the signed locations zi​,pi​ in Lecture I. All models are continuous-time and linear, with no actuator saturation in the plotted responses. Time is in seconds and signals are normalized. Settling times use the course’s 1% band.

Where we are, and where this lecture goes

Lecture II ended with a locus that did not go where the requirements wanted. A single gain only slides the poles along fixed branches. To move the branches themselves we must change the pole-zero pattern that generates them, and that means giving the controller dynamics of its own.

Two facts from Lecture I say what to expect, and FPE’s satellite examples in Lecture II made both concrete. Branches end at zeros, so a zero added in the left half-plane draws branches toward it: it pulled the double integrator’s locus off the imaginary axis onto a circle in the stable region. Branches start at poles, and an added pole spreads the asymptotes and pushes branches to the right, as the lead pole did when it was dragged in from −12 toward −4. Adding a zero is therefore the first move for improving a transient, and the controller that adds a zero is the PD controller from Chapter 4.

FPE opens §5.4 by naming the three compensations that have proved “particularly simple and effective”: lead, lag, and notch. Lead approximates PD and acts mainly to speed up a response; lag approximates PI and improves steady-state accuracy; notch stabilizes a lightly damped flexible mode. The first is today’s subject, the other two are Lecture IV’s. All three are built from one form,

Dc​(s)=Ks+ps+z​,

FPE’s Eq. (5.70), which is called lead compensation when z<p and lag compensation when z>p. The names come from the sinusoidal response, where a lead network’s output leads its input in phase; Part V makes that precise. The compensator is usually placed in series with the plant, which is what we do throughout. FPE notes that it can instead be placed in the feedback path, where it gives the same closed-loop poles but a different response to the reference. We will meet exactly that distinction in §5.

The story of this lecture:

  1. Look at FPE’s own opening picture: what the zero of K(s+2) does to the motor locus, and what happens when a pole is added behind it (§1).
  2. Ask for a specific pole pair on the motor and count how many degrees of angle the plant is short at that point (§2).
  3. Supply the missing angle with a PD zero, and discover that the response is not the one the pole formula promised (§3).
  4. Make the controller buildable by adding a pole, which turns PD into lead, and redo the design (§§4–5).
  5. Ask what the pole costs and what it buys (§6), then watch FPE iterate a lead design against real inequality specifications (§7).

1. FPE’s opening picture: a zero pulls the locus left, a pole pushes it back

FPE §5.4.1 begins where Lecture I began, with the normalized motor

G(s)=s(s+1)1​

under proportional control Dc​=K. Its locus is the pair of branches we know: along the real axis from 0 and −1 to the breakaway at −1/2, then straight up and down the line ℜs=−1/2. FPE then draws, on the same axes, the locus for PD control with Dc​=K(s+2), and the modified locus is a circle. The figure below reproduces FPE’s Fig. 5.22.

Why a circle, and where it sits. The locus for 1+K(s+2)/[s(s+1)]=0 must be a circle centered on the zero, by the same argument that gave Example 5.3’s circle in Lecture II. Put s=−z+rejθ, a point at distance r from the zero, and ask when K=−s(s+1)/(s+z) is real:

K=−rejθ(−z+rejθ)(1−z+rejθ)​=−[(1−2z)+rejθ+rz(z−1)​e−jθ].

The imaginary part is −[r−z(z−1)/r]sinθ, which vanishes for every θ exactly when r2=z(z−1). On that circle,

K=(2z−1)−2z(z−1)​cosθ,

which is positive for all θ once z>1. So the complex part of the PD locus is a circle of radius z(z−1)​ centered on the zero at −z. For z=2 the radius is 2​: the branches leave the real axis at −2+2​=−0.586 when K=3−22​, sweep around the circle, and rejoin it at −2−2​=−3.414 when K=3+22​. (For z=1 the radius is zero: the zero cancels the motor pole and the circle collapses. Lecture II’s circle for K(s+z)/s2 had radius z because the second plant pole sat at the origin instead of at −1.)

Why this matters. Suppose a speed-of-response requirement calls for ωn​≈2 rad/s. With proportional control the characteristic polynomial is s2+s+K, so ωn​=2 needs K=4 and leaves 2ζωn​=1, that is, ζ=1/4: the standard pair would overshoot by 44%. With PD control the polynomial is

s(s+1)+K(s+2)=s2+(1+K)s+2K,

so ωn​=2 needs K=2 and gives 2ζωn​=3, that is, ζ=3/4, with the pair at −1.5±1.323j. FPE’s conclusion, in the book’s words: “we have ‘compensated’ the given dynamics by using Dc​(s)=K(s+2).” The same radius, three times the damping ratio. (Whether the response is as good as ζ=0.75 suggests is a separate question, because the closed-loop numerator now contains the zero; §3 takes that up.)

Now add a pole. Pure derivative control amplifies sensor noise and must be approximated. FPE’s approximation is the lead compensator

Dc​(s)=Ks+ps+2​,

and the right panel shows the loci for p=20 and p=10 beside the PD circle, reproducing FPE’s Fig. 5.23. FPE’s reading of the picture: for small gains, before the real root departing from −p approaches −2, the loci with lead compensation are almost identical to the PD locus. The pole’s effect is to lower the damping, but for the early part of the locus the effect is not great if p>10. You can see the mechanism from Lecture I’s rules. The PD locus had one branch to infinity, along the negative real axis. The lead locus has n−m=2 branches to infinity, along vertical asymptotes through σa​=(0−1−p+2)/2, which is −9.5 for p=20 and −4.5 for p=10. The farther the pole, the farther out the asymptotes, and the longer the branches follow the circle before peeling off toward them.

FPE records the working rules of thumb: place the zero in the neighborhood of the closed-loop ωn​ set by the rise-time or settling-time requirement, and place the pole at 5 to 20 times the zero (7th ed.); the 8th edition’s procedure says to keep the ratio p/z below about 25. The pole location is a compromise. Too close to the zero and the locus “does not move as much from its uncompensated shape, and the zero is not as successful in doing its job.” Too far left and the magnification of sensor noise at the controller output is too great, so the actuator “can be overheated by noise energy in the control signal.” With p→∞ the lead becomes pure PD.

Trial and error is how FPE places the zero and pole, and it is how Example 5.11 proceeds in §7. Between here and there we do something more exact: ask for a particular pole pair and compute where the zero and pole must go. That calculation is the angle condition from Lecture I used in reverse.

2. Start with a pole-placement request: the angle deficiency

For G=1/[s(s+1)], request sd​=−2+2j and its conjugate. Thus

ωn​=8​,ζ=2​1​,(s−sd​)(s−sd​​)=s2+4s+8.

The motor’s proportional-gain locus has its complex branches at ℜs=−1/2 and cannot reach this point. Lecture I’s angle condition says why in a way that also says what to do about it.

At sd​, the pole-vector angles are

arg(sd​)=arg(−2+2j)=135∘,arg(sd​+1)=arg(−1+2j)=116.565∘.

The plant phase at sd​ is therefore −(135∘+116.565∘)=−251.565∘. For sd​ to be a closed-loop pole for some positive gain, the total loop phase must be −180∘ (or −180∘ plus a multiple of 360∘). The controller must contribute

ϕc​=251.565∘−180∘=71.565∘​.

This is the angle deficiency: the angle needed to put this particular point on the compensated locus. It is not a phase margin, which will be defined using sinusoidal frequency response in Part V.

Read 71.565∘ as a budget. The two plant arrows at sd​ use up 251.565∘ of negative angle, which is 71.565∘ more than the 180∘ the angle condition allows. Something in the controller must give that angle back. An arrow from a zero adds to the total; an arrow from a pole subtracts. So the controller needs at least one zero, placed so that its arrow to sd​ makes exactly the missing angle, and any pole we add later will have to be paid for with a larger zero angle.

The standard no-zero second-order formula predicts 4.32% overshoot for this pair. Treat that as a prediction to test, not as the guaranteed overshoot of the compensated system.

3. PD design by the angle condition

Locate the zero, then set the gain

The simplest controller with a zero is PD. Let Dc​=K(s+z)=kD​s+kP​. A single zero must supply 71.565∘, so its arrow to sd​, which is the vector sd​+z=(z−2)+2j, must make that angle with the real axis:

arg(sd​+z)=71.565∘,tan71.565∘=z−22​=3.

Consequently z−2=2/3 and z=8/3. The zero sits at −8/3, just to the left of the target’s real part, and its arrow rises steeply to sd​. The magnitude condition K∣sd​+z∣=∣sd​(sd​+1)∣ gives

K=∣sd​+8/3∣∣sd​(sd​+1)∣​=∣2/3+2j∣∣−2−6j∣​=40/9​40​​=3.

Hence

Dc​(s)=3(s+38​)=3s+8​,kD​=3,kP​=8.

Check by expanding the characteristic equation:

s(s+1)+3s+8=s2+4s+8.

Coefficient matching gives the same result: 1+kD​=4 and kP​=8. For this second-order loop we could have skipped the geometry and matched coefficients directly. The geometry is still the thing to learn, because it is what survives when the plant is fifth order and the coefficients cannot be matched by hand.

What response did we actually design?

TPD​(s)=1+Dc​GDc​G​=s2+4s+83s+8​.

The numerator is not 8. The controller zero at −8/3 is now a closed-loop zero, and the time-domain lecture said what a left-half-plane zero near the dominant poles does to a step response. Here it is explicitly. For a unit step,

Y(s)=s(s2+4s+8)3s+8​=s1​+s2+4s+8−s−1​,

which you can confirm by recombining: (s2+4s+8)+s(−s−1)=3s+8. Complete the square and split the second term into the two standard shapes:

(s+2)2+4−s−1​=−(s+2)2+4s+2​+(s+2)2+41​,

so

y(t)=1−e−2tcos2t+21​e−2tsin2t​,t≥0.

dtd​[−e−2tcos2t]=e−2t(2cos2t+2sin2t),dtd​[21​e−2tsin2t]=e−2t(cos2t−sin2t),

y˙​(t)=e−2t(3cos2t+sin2t).

The first maximum occurs where tan2t=−3 in the second quadrant:

2tp​=π−tan−13,tp​=0.9463 s,cos2tp​=−10​1​,sin2tp​=10​3​.

Substituting into y(tp​)−1,

Mp​=e−2tp​(10​1​+210​3​)=410​​e−(π−tan−13)≈0.1191.

The PD design overshoots by about 11.91%, not 4.32%. The poles are exactly where we asked. The response is not what the pole formula promised, because that formula assumed no finite zero.

Why the zero raises the overshoot

There is a clean way to see the mechanism. Factor the numerator:

TPD​(s)=s2+4s+88​(1+83s​),

so the PD step response is the standard pair’s step response plus 3/8 of its time derivative. The standard response is rising steeply early on, and adding a fraction of that slope lifts the curve: it rises sooner and overshoots more. A zero near the dominant poles always does this to some degree, and the closer the zero, the larger the effect. The poles determine the shape of each mode; the zero determines how much of each mode appears. This is the derivative decomposition from the time-domain lecture, meeting us again in a design we chose ourselves.

Why an ideal PD is incomplete as hardware

The second problem with Dc​=3s+8 is not in the output but in the command. Its magnitude grows without bound as frequency increases, so differentiating a noisy measurement can demand large actuator commands. Differentiating a step reference demands an impulse: the command is u=8e+3e˙, and a reference step produces a jump in e before the strictly proper plant output can move, so 3e˙ contains 3δ(t). For this example the impulse area in u is kD​=3 times the step amplitude. Derivative on the measured output avoids that reference impulse but still needs filtering for noise. Recall the derivative-kick and filtered-derivative discussion in Chapter 4; the lead compensator is that filter, designed from the root locus.

4. From PD to lead: add the pole, redo the design

The fix is to add a pole far enough out that it tames the high-frequency gain but close enough in to be cheap in angle. A pole subtracts angle at sd​, so after adding one the zero must supply more than 71.565∘, which moves the zero closer to the target. Choose a finite-pole controller

Dc​(s)=Ks+ps+z​,p>z>0.

Three unknowns, two conditions: the angle condition fixes one relation between z and p, and the magnitude condition then fixes K. One parameter is free, and Nise’s Fig. 9.25 makes the point graphically: an infinite number of lead compensators pass through the design point. We will choose the zero, solve for the pole, then set the gain. This is Ogata’s §6–6 procedure and Nise’s §9.3 procedure in the same order.

The general construction

Let the target be sd​=−σ+jωd​ in the upper left half-plane and let ϕc​ be the positive angle the controller must supply. For a chosen zero parameter z, the arrow from −z to sd​ is sd​+z=(z−σ)+jωd​, so

θz​=arg(sd​+z)=atan2(ωd​,z−σ).

The pole’s arrow must subtract whatever the zero oversupplies:

θp​=θz​−ϕc​.

The arrow from a real pole at −p to sd​ is (p−σ)+jωd​, with angle θp​, so tanθp​=ωd​/(p−σ) and

p=σ+ωd​cotθp​​.

Then the magnitude condition gives

K=∣sd​+z∣∣sd​∣∣sd​+1∣∣sd​+p∣​​

for our motor, or K=1/∣G(sd​)(sd​+z)/(sd​+p)∣ in general.

The construction also contains a feasibility test. The arrow from any real pole to a point in the upper half-plane has an angle strictly between 0∘ and 180∘, so we need θp​>0, that is, θz​>ϕc​: the zero alone must oversupply the required angle. If the chosen zero supplies less than ϕc​, no real pole can rescue it, because a pole can only subtract. At θp​→0 the pole runs off to −∞ and the lead degenerates into the ideal PD of §3. And p>z, the lead condition, is the same as θp​<θz​, which holds automatically when ϕc​>0. If a computed p is inadmissible, change the zero or the controller structure; do not keep a numerically real but physically unintended pole.

Our numbers

Choose z=2 for straightforward geometry. At sd​=−2+2j, the zero vector is sd​+2=2j, so θz​=90∘. The controller pole must remove the excess angle:

θp​=90∘−71.565∘=18.435∘,tan18.435∘=31​.

Thus

p=2+2cot18.435∘=2+2⋅3=8.

The magnitude condition now gives

K=∣sd​+2∣∣sd​∣∣sd​+1∣∣sd​+8∣​=28​5​40​​=240​40​​=20,

using ∣sd​∣=8​, ∣sd​+1∣=∣−1+2j∣=5​, ∣sd​+8∣=∣6+2j∣=40​, and ∣sd​+2∣=2. Therefore

Dc​(s)=20s+8s+2​​.

Check the gain including every factor. Numerically G(sd​)(sd​+2)/(sd​+8)=−1/20, so the selected gain makes the loop exactly −1 at sd​, which is the angle condition and the magnitude condition at once.

5. Verify every pole and the complete response

The third pole

The loop is now third order, so the gain that put two poles at −2±2j put a third pole somewhere too. Clearing denominators,

P(s)=s(s+1)(s+8)+20(s+2)=s3+9s2+28s+40.

We know two roots, so divide by s2+4s+8:

P(s)=(s+5)(s2+4s+8).

(Check the constant term: 5⋅8=40; the s term: 5⋅4+8=28; the s2 term: 4+5=9.) The additional pole is −5. It is only 2.5 times farther left than the target pair, and the zero at −2 is nearby; a pure second-order approximation is not justified without checking.

The complete step response

Y(s)=s(s+5)(s2+4s+8)20(s+2)​=s1​+s+5A​+s2+4s+8Bs+C​.

The residue at s=−5 is

A=(−5)[(−5)2+4(−5)+8]20(−5+2)​=(−5)(13)−60​=1312​.

For B and C, compare the large-s behavior. The left side decays like 20/s3, so the 1/s and 1/s2 coefficients of the right side must vanish:

1+A+B=0 ⇒ B=−1325​,−5A+C−4B=0 ⇒ C=1360​−13100​=−1340​.

Rewrite the quadratic term in the two standard shapes,

Bs+C=−1325​(s+2)+1310​,

and invert:

y(t)=1+1312​e−5t−1325​e−2tcos2t+135​e−2tsin2t​.

Two checks catch sign errors before any plot. At t=0, 1+12/13−25/13=0. The initial slope is −60/13+50/13+10/13=0, as it must be for a plant of relative degree two driven by a finite command. The real pole’s residue is 12/13, nearly one. That pole is not negligible merely because it lies to the left of the pair; its nearby zero at −2 does not cancel it in Y/R.

The first row is proportional control with the same kP​, for reference; its poles are −1/2±2.78j. The middle two rows share the pole pair −2±2j and differ in their numerators and companion poles. The last row is the derivative acting on the measured output rather than on the error: the same gains, the same characteristic polynomial s2+4s+8, but a numerator of 8 and so the textbook 4.32%. That is FPE’s remark from the §5.4 introduction about compensation in the feedback path, and Lecture IV’s §9 develops it as a root-locus design. Equal pole pairs do not mean equal step responses.

Reading the two loci

The two loci in the figure are worth a minute. The PD locus has two poles and one zero, and its complex part is a circle centered on the zero at −8/3 with radius (8/3)(5/3)​=2.108, by §1’s formula; the target pair sits on that circle, and every point on it is reachable by some gain. The lead locus has three poles and one zero, so two branches go to infinity along vertical asymptotes through σa​=(0−1−8+2)/2=−7/2, and the target pair sits where those branches pass through −2±2j. The added pole has opened the circle into a pair of branches that head for infinity, exactly as in §1’s Fig. 5.23. Both loci pass through the target; they do not pass through it with the same companions.

Steady-state tracking

The locus does not show tracking accuracy, so check it separately. For the lead, Kv​=lims→0​sDc​G=Dc​(0)=20⋅2/8=5, so the unit-ramp error is 0.2. For the PD, Kv​=Dc​(0)=8 and the ramp error is 0.125. Both loops are Type 1 and track a constant reference with zero steady-state error. The lead’s lower Kv​ is the first of its costs; §6 has the others, and Lecture IV shows how to buy the accuracy back.

6. What does the lead pole buy?

If the PD gave less overshoot, a faster rise, and better tracking, why bother with the lead? The answer is in what each controller does at high frequency, where sensor noise lives and where the ideal derivative asks for an impulse.

High-frequency gain, and the lead as a filtered PD

For our lead design,

Dc​(0)=Kpz​=5,∣s∣→∞lim​Dc​(s)=K=20.

The high-frequency gain is finite, but it is not zero. To see the filtered derivative explicitly, split the lead into a constant and a strictly proper part:

Ks+ps+z​=Kp(s+p)z(s+p)+(p−z)s​=Kpz​+K(1−pz​)s+ps​.

Since s/(s+p)=(s/p)/(1+s/p), this is

Dc​(s)=kP​+1+Tf​skD​s​,kP​=pKz​,kD​=p2K(p−z)​,Tf​=p1​.

Here kP​=5, kD​=1.875, and the derivative filter time constant is Tf​=1/8 s. In the notation of the PID implementation lecture, where Tf​=TD​/N and the derivative’s high-frequency gain is kD​/Tf​=kP​N, this lead has N=3: a heavier filter than the usual 5 to 20, which is why its high-frequency gain is only four times its proportional gain. These are not the ideal PD’s gains, kP​=8 and kD​=3; the lead was redesigned to recover the target roots after adding a pole, and the redesign changed every parameter.

Control effort and noise

The reference-to-command path is

RU​=1+Dc​GDc​​=Dc​S=s(s+1)(s+8)+20(s+2)20(s+2)s(s+1)​.

For a unit step, the initial-value theorem gives u(0+)=lims→∞​Dc​(s)=20, because G→0 at high frequency and S→1. The lead replaces the PD’s impulse with a finite jump of 20 times the step amplitude, which is still large; it is the price of asking a motor whose natural time constant is one second to settle in two. The final command for a constant reference is Dc​(0)S(0)=0, since the loop is Type 1; a constant load would need a constant torque, which this calculation does not include.

With sensor noise N added to the measurement, U=Dc​(R−Y−N), so U/N=−Dc​S. Its high-frequency limit is −20. The lead caps differentiation; it does not remove noise amplification. Include sensor noise and actuator limits in any physical implementation.

A common misconception

Moving a lead pole farther left while holding K fixed does not preserve its low-frequency gain, since Dc​(0)=Kz/p falls as p grows. To approach a fixed ideal PD kD​(s+z), write the lead as Dc​=kD​p(s+z)/(s+p) and let p grow: the DC gain kD​z is then fixed, and the high-frequency gain kD​p grows without bound. That is the sense in which “a lead with a far pole approximates PD,” and it shows what the approximation costs.

7. FPE Example 5.11: iterate against actual specifications

So far we chose a pole location and hit it exactly. Real requirements are inequalities on the response, and the designer iterates: pick a target region, design, simulate, adjust. FPE’s Example 5.11 shows that loop on the same motor, and it ends with the revised compensator that Lecture IV builds on.

From specifications to a target region

FPE requests at most 20% overshoot and at most 0.3 s rise time for G=1/[s(s+1)]. These are different requirements from our exact pole-placement exercise. Using Chapter 3’s estimates, FPE translates them: the overshoot bound suggests ζ≥0.5, and tr​≈1.8/ωn​≤0.3 s suggests ωn​≥6 rad/s. “To provide some margin,” the book shoots for ζ≥0.5 and ωn​≥7 rad/s. That is a wedge and a circle in the s-plane, Lecture II’s picture, and the motor’s proportional locus at ℜs=−1/2 does not enter it.

First iteration: K=70, p=10

Considering the loci of Fig. 5.23, FPE first tries the zero at −2 with the pole at −10,

Dc,1​(s)=70s+10s+2​.

The characteristic polynomial and its roots are

P1​=s(s+1)(s+10)+70(s+2)=s3+11s2+80s+140,s=−2.3449, −4.3276±6.4013j.

The complex pair has ζ=0.560 and ωn​=7.73 rad/s, which satisfies the goals based on the initial estimates. The third pole is at −2.34 (FPE rounds to −2.4), so close to the lead zero at −2 that, in FPE’s words, “the overshoot should not be increased very much from the second-order case.” The simulated step response says otherwise: 22.27% overshoot, and the design fails the overshoot requirement by a small amount. FPE’s diagnosis is the one we reached in §3: lead compensation in the forward path typically increases the step-response overshoot “because the zero of the compensation has a differentiating effect.” The 10–90% rise time, 0.1953 s, meets its specification easily.

Second iteration: K=91, p=13

More damping is needed. The 8th edition’s guidance: “it is best to increase p in order to increase damping, providing the p/z ratio stays below approximately 25,” and since not much more damping is needed, a modest increase from 10 to 13. Moving the pole left pulls the locus left, as §1 showed, and the gain is reselected on the new locus:

Dc,2​(s)=91s+13s+2​​,P2​=s3+14s2+104s+182.

Now the poles are −2.3855 and −5.8072±6.5245j, a pair with ζ=0.665 and ωn​=8.73 rad/s. The full response gives 16.86% overshoot and 0.1895 s 10–90% rise time, meeting both requirements; FPE reports about 17%. Its 1% settling time is about 1.395 s, against 1.433 s for the first design. Here overshoot, rise time, and settling time all improve. The cost appears in the control effort: the initial command demand rises from 70 to 91 times the step amplitude, as the figure’s right panel shows.

For either candidate, stability can be confirmed without finding the roots. With Dc​=K(s+2)/(s+p) the characteristic polynomial is

P=s3+(p+1)s2+(p+K)s+2K,

and the cubic Routh condition is positive coefficients plus (p+1)(p+K)>2K: 11⋅80>140 and 14⋅104>182. Both pass with a wide margin.

What closes the design argument

In both designs the real pole lies closer to the imaginary axis than the complex pair. It is slow, not fast. Its nearby zero reduces its reference-step contribution without removing it, which is why the second-order estimate was optimistic. The simulation, not a declaration that the pair is “dominant,” closes the design argument.

FPE’s design procedure for lead compensation

The 8th edition closes §5.4.1 with a procedure that summarizes what we have done. In outline:

  1. Determine where the closed-loop roots need to be to meet the speed and damping requirements: limits on ζ and ωn​, or on tr​, ts​, and Mp​.
  2. Draw the root locus against K with no compensation.
  3. If more damping is required, place the lead zero at roughly 1/4 to 1 times the desired ωn​ (or 1/tr​), and place the pole at 5 to 25 times the zero.
  4. Examine the resulting locus and adjust: decrease z if less damping is needed; increase p or decrease z if more is needed; keep p/z as low as possible to limit the amplification of sensor noise.
  5. Once the locus passes through an acceptable region, select K to place the closed-loop roots.
  6. Verify every time-domain specification against the unit-step response; if one fails, adjust the target region and return to step 2.
  7. Check the steady-state error requirements, if any; if no admissible K meets them, add integral control or a lag compensator. That is Lecture IV.

Steps 3 and 4 are FPE’s trial-and-error placement; §4’s construction replaces them with a calculation when a specific pole pair is the goal. Step 6 is the one most often skipped.

What to take away

  • To move the branches, change the pole-zero pattern. A left-half-plane zero pulls the locus toward it; an added pole pushes it away. On the motor, K(s+2) turns the vertical branches into a circle and triples the damping ratio available at ωn​=2.
  • At a target pole, total the plant’s arrow angles. The shortfall from 180∘ is the angle the controller must supply, and a zero supplies it.
  • PD places the poles exactly, but the zero appears in the closed-loop numerator and changes the response: the standard pair’s 4.32% became 11.91%. Poles set the modes; zeros set how much of each mode you get.
  • A lead compensator adds a pole to cap the high-frequency gain. It costs angle, so the zero moves in and the gain is redesigned. Choose the zero, solve θp​=θz​−ϕc​ for the pole, set K by magnitude, and check that θz​>ϕc​ so a real pole exists.
  • The added pole is not far from the pair, so find every pole and check the complete response. The lead’s third pole at −5 carries a residue of 12/13.
  • A lead is a filtered PD with kP​=Kz/p, kD​=K(p−z)/p2, and Tf​=1/p. Its finite high-frequency gain K is also its initial step command and its noise gain.
  • Design to the actual specification by iteration and simulation, as FPE does. “The pair is dominant” is a hypothesis to test, not a conclusion.

Review questions

  1. Why is the required angle 71.565∘, rather than the magnitude of the plant’s principal phase angle?
  2. In Dc​=K(s+z), which parameter is the proportional gain?
  3. Why did adding the lead pole require redesigning the gain?
  4. The lead and PD share −2±2j. Why do they overshoot differently?
  5. What changes in the reference transfer function when the derivative acts on the measurement?

Optional practice

Keep the target −2+2j, choose another zero location, and use §4’s construction (Ogata 5th ed. §6–6’s angle-deficiency procedure) to find a valid lead pole. Find the gain and remaining pole, then compare the complete response and initial control demand. Not every arbitrary zero choice produces a finite real pole with p>z>0. For this target, z>8/3 gives less than the required 71.565∘ from the zero alone, so adding a real pole cannot supply the missing positive angle. For example, z=3 supplies only 63.435∘. At z=8/3, the required pole angle is zero: the finite-pole construction reaches its ideal-PD limit.
