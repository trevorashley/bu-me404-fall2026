BU ENG ME404: Dynamics and Control of Mechanical Systems, Fall 2026

Bode Plot Techniques

Sources: Franklin, Powell, and Emami-Naeini, Feedback Control of Dynamic Systems, 8th edition, §6.1.1, Examples 6.3–6.6; cross-checked against the 7th edition, printed pp. 317–329. The example order and transfer functions follow FPE. Local corrections are identified where needed. Supplementary corroboration: Ogata, Modern Control Engineering, 5th edition, §7-2 (factor rules and second-order resonance).

Prerequisites: Frequency Response, complex-number magnitude and angle, transfer functions, and MATLAB. No Fourier transform or complex integration is needed.

Objectives: Normalize a transfer function; add the magnitude and phase contributions of its factors; construct Examples 6.3–6.6; and explain why magnitude alone cannot locate a zero in the right half-plane.

For a stable system’s settled sinusoidal response, an input Asinωt produces A∣G(jω)∣sin(ωt+ϕ), where M=∣G(jω)∣ and ϕ=argG(jω). We can also evaluate an open-loop frequency response containing integrators for every ω>0, without asserting that its arbitrary transient decays. Here L denotes the full loop, including every gain, controller, plant, and sensor: L=KDc​GH when K is written separately. All examples below already include their gains. Feedback is negative; frequency is in rad/s. Angles inside sinusoidal arguments and complex exponentials are in radians; convert a displayed degree angle by multiplying by π/180. Displayed Bode phases and the inverse tangent formulas below use degrees (MATLAB atand and atan2d).

1. Logarithms and the three factor classes

A logarithmic frequency axis gives equal space to equal frequency ratios. A decade is a factor of ten; an octave is a factor of two. Thus 0.1 to 1 and 10 to 100 span the same horizontal distance. Decibels turn multiplication of magnitudes into addition.

Under equal reference conditions, power is proportional to amplitude squared. If M=A2​/A1​, then

10log10​P1​P2​​=10log10​M2=20log10​M≡MdB​.

For a dimensional transfer function, use 20log10​(∣G∣/Gref​) with a stated reference having the same units; writing dB without that reference would take the logarithm of a dimensional quantity. The examples use normalized numerical transfer functions and unit magnitude as reference. A factor of 10 in amplitude is 20 dB; a factor of 2 is approximately 6.02 dB.

Write each complex factor as Mk​ejϕk​. Multiplication gives (M1​M2​)ej(ϕ1​+ϕ2​), so

20log10​(M1​M2​)=20log10​M1​+20log10​M2​.

Division subtracts both dB magnitudes and phases. Angles differing by 360∘ represent the same complex number, but a plotted phase should follow a continuous branch.

Normalize into a positive scale K0​, an integer power sn, first-order factors 1+s/ωb​, and second-order factors 1+2ζs/ωn​+(s/ωn​)2. For the stable factors here, ωb​,ωn​,ζ>0. Keep a fixed negative sign separately: it contributes 180∘, even though its magnitude is one. K0​ generally differs from the coefficient in an unnormalized pole-zero expression.

Class 1: gain and powers of frequency

Substitute s=jω into K0​sn. Since ∣jω∣=ω and arg(jω)=90∘,

M=K0​ωn,MdB​=20log10​K0​+20nlog10​ω,ϕ=90n∘.

Multiplying frequency by ten changes magnitude by 20n dB. This is a straight line of slope 20n dB/decade. Negative n describes poles at the origin; positive n describes zeros there.

Class 2: a real pole or zero

Let r=ω/ωb​. For a zero, 1+jr has real part 1 and imaginary part r, hence

M=1+r2​,ϕ=tan−1r.

For r≪1, M≈1 and ϕ→0; for r≫1, M≈r and ϕ→90∘. The magnitude asymptote therefore changes from slope 0 to +20 dB/decade at ωb​. At the corner r=1, M=2​, giving 20log10​2​=3.0103 dB above the asymptote and ϕ=45∘.

A pole 1/(1+jr) has reciprocal magnitude and negative phase. Its high-frequency slope is −20 dB/decade and its corner correction is −3.0103 dB. A convenient straight phase approximation for a zero is

ϕapprox​=⎩⎨⎧​0,45(1+log10​r)∘,90∘,​r≤0.1,0.1<r<10,r≥10.​

Reverse the sign for a pole. The exact arctangent is smooth; the ramp is only a sketching aid.

Class 3: a complex pole or zero pair

Put r=ω/ωn​. Substitution gives

Q(jω)=1−r2+j2ζr,∣Q∣=(1−r2)2+4ζ2r2​,argQ=atan2(2ζr,1−r2).

The two-argument angle function retains the quadrant when 1−r2 becomes negative. For r≪1, Q≈1; for r≫1, Q≈−r2, so the magnitude slope changes from 0 to +40 dB/decade and continuous phase goes from 0 to 180∘. At r=1, Q=j2ζ, so its magnitude is 2ζ and phase is 90∘. For 1/Q, invert magnitude and negate phase: slope −40 dB/decade, corner magnitude 1/(2ζ), and phase from 0 to −180∘.

The following comparison shows the exact component curves and their magnitude asymptotes at unit corner frequency. The pair uses ζ=0.1.

The sharp pair transition differs substantially from the first-order ramp. Its shape depends on damping; do not apply a first-order phase ramp indiscriminately to a complex pair.

Watch out: s+10=10(1+s/10). Dropping 10 preserves the pole location but shifts the entire magnitude plot by 20 dB.

2. FPE’s eight-step construction

  1. Rewrite the transfer function in normalized Bode form.
  2. Identify K0​ωn and locate its low-frequency line: its extension passes through K0​ at ω=1.
  3. Visit corners in ascending order. Add +20 dB/decade per real zero or −20 per real pole, and twice those changes for a pair.
  4. Correct magnitude near the corners using the component curves: ±3.0103 dB for a real factor, and 2ζ or its reciprocal at a pair’s natural frequency.
  5. Start phase at 90n∘, including any fixed sign.
  6. Mark a stepwise phase guide: +90 degrees per real LHP zero, −90 per real pole, and ±180 per pair.
  7. Position each individual smooth phase transition relative to that guide.
  8. Add the individual phases to obtain the composite curve.

FPE’s stepwise phase guide is a way to organize the eventual phase changes; it is not a physical discontinuity. Individual transitions overlap, so the composite need not reach an intermediate guide level.

FPE Rule 4 describes a peak or valley at the break point; that wording is an approximation to the neighborhood of the feature, not the exact extremum location. A pair’s exact value at ωn​ is not generally its extremum. To locate the inverse pair’s peak, minimize its squared denominator,

D(r)=(1−r2)2+4ζ2r2=1+(4ζ2−2)r2+r4.

Differentiating term by term gives

D′(r)=2(4ζ2−2)r+4r3=4r(r2+2ζ2−1).

The positive stationary point is rr​=1−2ζ2​, which exists only for 0<ζ<1/2​. Here the derivative changes from negative to positive, so D is minimized. Substitution gives D(rr​)=4ζ2(1−ζ2) and the peak Mr​=1/[2ζ1−ζ2​]. For larger damping the inverse pair decreases from its DC value without a resonant peak. Additional factors can shift or remove a full transfer function’s local extrema.

3. Example 6.3: real poles and zero

Follow all eight steps for

L=s(s+10)(s+50)2000(s+0.5)​=s(1+s/10)(1+s/50)2(1+s/0.5)​.

Steps 1–2: The scale is 2000(0.5)/(10⋅50)=2 and n=−1, so the initial magnitude line is 2/ω with slope −20 dB/decade. Step 3: The corners are 0.5, 10, and 50. The zero adds 20 and the poles each subtract 20 dB/decade. Continuity at each corner gives

A(ω)=⎩⎨⎧​2/ω,4,40/ω,2000/ω2,​ω<0.5,0.5≤ω<10,10≤ω<50,ω≥50.​

Step 4: Apply the individual +3.0103 dB zero correction at 0.5 and −3.0103 dB pole corrections at 10 and 50. Other factors are also active there, so the exact composite is obtained by multiplying all factors:

M=ω1+(ω/10)2​1+(ω/50)2​21+(2ω)2​​.

Steps 5–6: Start at −90∘. The stepwise guide has successive levels −90,0,−90,−180 degrees. Steps 7–8: Add the integrator and three smooth phase curves:

ϕ=−90∘+tan−1(2ω)−tan−1(ω/10)−tan−1(ω/50).

The limiting phase is −90+90−90−90=−180∘. The plot separates the individual contributions and then combines them.

The zero lifts the phase before the poles pull it down. Their transitions overlap, so the exact phase does not reach the guide’s intermediate 0-degree level.

Don’t make this mistake: The extended 2/ω line passes through 2 at 1 rad/s. The actual curve need not pass there because the zero has already changed its shape.

4. Example 6.4: complex poles

Normalize the quadratic’s constant 4:

L=s(s2+0.4s+4)10​=s[(s/2)2+2(0.1)(s/2)+1]2.5​.

Thus ωn​=2, ζ=0.1, and the magnitude asymptote is 2.5/ω below 2 and 10/ω3 above 2. The slopes change from −20 to −60 dB/decade. Direct substitution and addition of the integrator’s phase give

M=ω(1−ω2/4)2+(0.1ω)2​2.5​,ϕ=−90∘−atan2(0.1ω,1−ω2/4).

At ω=2, the inverse pair contributes 1/(2ζ)=5; hence M=(2.5/2)5=6.25 and ϕ=−180∘. The limiting phases are −90 and −270 degrees. The pair alone peaks at 20.98​ rad/s, but the integrator varies with frequency too; this is not the exact local peak frequency of the complete loop. Indeed its magnitude diverges as ω→0, so a resonant bump is not a global maximum.

The narrow bump comes from the lightly damped pair. Its 2 rad/s marker is the natural-frequency corner, not a claim about the full-loop extremum.

5. Example 6.5: satellite with flexible appendages

FPE models coupled masses with force applied to, and position measured on, the same mass. The supplied loop transfer function is

L=s2(s2/4+0.01s+1)0.01(s2+0.01s+1)​.

For the usual free two-mass realization, with driven/measured mass m1​ and other mass m2​, the undamped natural-frequency ratio is ωp​/ωz​=(m1​+m2​)/m1​​. Equal masses give 2​, whereas the supplied model gives 2 (corresponding to m2​=3m1​ in that realization).

Watch out: Both editions describe equal masses, but their plotted model has different pole/zero spacing. Use the displayed model for Examples 6.5 and 6.6; a scalar gain cannot change that spacing.

The numerator pair has ωz​=1, 2ζz​ωz​=0.01, hence ζz​=0.005. The denominator pair has ωp​=2 and 2ζp​/ωp​=0.01, hence ζp​=0.01. The double integrator gives the initial slope −40 dB/decade and phase −180 degrees. The zero pair adds 40 at 1, and the pole pair subtracts 40 at 2, giving

A(ω)=⎩⎨⎧​0.01/ω2,0.01,0.04/ω2,​ω<1,1≤ω<2,ω≥2.​

Substitute s=jω into both pairs, take their magnitudes, and subtract denominator phase:

M=ω20.01​(1−ω2/4)2+(0.01ω)2(1−ω2)2+(0.01ω)2​​,

ϕ=−180∘+atan2(0.01ω,1−ω2)−atan2(0.01ω,1−ω2/4).

The isolated zero correction is 2ζz​=0.01, and the isolated pole correction is 1/(2ζp​)=50. Applied alone to the 0.01 asymptote these predict 10−4 and 0.5. But the neighboring factor is not yet asymptotic:

M(1)=0.010.752+0.012​0.01​≈0.0001333215,

M(2)=40.01​0.02(−3)2+0.022​​≈0.3750083.

The hand construction below shows the asymptotes, isolated correction estimates, and their full-model corner values. These corner samples are not the exact notch or peak locations.

The zero pair creates an antiresonance, a deep response notch; the pole pair creates a resonance bump. The phase guide rises from −180 toward 0 between 1 and 2, then returns to −180. Nonzero damping makes the actual phase continuous.

Why this matters: A lightly damped flexible structure can have both a deep notch and a sharp peak. A coarse frequency sweep can miss either and give a misleading picture of the plant.

6. Example 6.6: MATLAB verification of the same model

This separate example checks both the hand construction and the adequacy of the sampling. With Control System Toolbox:

s = tf('s');
L = 0.01*(s^2 + 0.01*s + 1)/(s^2*(s^2/4 + 0.01*s + 1));
w = sort(unique([logspace(-2,2,1000), ...
    linspace(0.95,1.05,4001), linspace(1.9,2.1,4001)]));
[mag,phase] = bode(L,w);
mag = squeeze(mag);
phase = squeeze(phase);
subplot(2,1,1); semilogx(w,20*log10(mag)); grid on
xlabel('Frequency (rad/s)'); ylabel('Magnitude (dB)');
subplot(2,1,2); semilogx(w,phase); grid on
xlabel('Frequency (rad/s)'); ylabel('Phase (deg)');

bode returns linear magnitude; a standard Bode magnitude plot displays 20log10​(mag). For this single-input/single-output model, the returned arrays have singleton input and output dimensions. squeeze removes those dimensions, leaving a vector indexed by frequency. sort(unique(...)) merges the logarithmic base grid with dense local samples into an ascending grid without duplicates.

The comparison includes close-ups of each flexible feature beside the complete response.

The sketch correctly predicts the trends but its isolated corner corrections omit overlap. Refining the local grid and independently locating extrema confirms that the narrow features have been resolved. Run report = frequency_response_bode(true) to reproduce all figures, or use false to compute the numerical report without exporting figures. Run results = verify_frequency_response_bode() for independent numerical checks; see the demo README.

7. Nonminimum-phase comparison

A right-half-plane (RHP) zero has positive real part. The following pair from FPE differs only in the zero location:

G1​=10s+10s+1​,G2​=10s+10s−1​.

Source correction: The local 8th-edition extract accidentally prints s+1 in both numerators. The 7th edition correctly gives s−1 for G2​, consistent with the RHP zero and the +180-degree branch in Fig. 6.12.

Because ∣jω+1∣=∣jω−1∣=ω2+1​, both magnitudes are

M=10ω2+100ω2+1​​.

For ω>0, the numerator of G1​ is in quadrant I; that of G2​ is in quadrant II. Subtract the denominator angle to obtain continuous phases

ϕ1​=tan−1ω−tan−1(ω/10),ϕ2​=180∘−tan−1ω−tan−1(ω/10).

The second curve starts at +180 degrees because G2​(0)=−1, whereas G1​(0)=+1. Both approach 0 degrees at high frequency. Replacing s−1 by 1−s would change the system’s sign and phase. A phase branch shifted by 360 degrees is equivalent if used consistently.

The comparison reproduces the meaning of FPE Fig. 6.12 while labeling the DC signs.

The ratio G2​/G1​=(s−1)/(s+1) has unit magnitude for all positive frequencies, yet contributes additional phase. G1​ is minimum phase here: its stable poles and zero lie in the LHP. The RHP zero makes G2​ nonminimum phase.

Connection: Root locations encode information that magnitude alone loses. A right-half-plane zero leaves this magnitude unchanged but changes the phase available for feedback design.

A Bode construction combines normalized factors: add their dB magnitudes and continuous phases, then use exact evaluation to check nearby corners and narrow flexible features.
