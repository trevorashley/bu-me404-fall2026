The Root-Locus Design Method I — From a Gain to a Family of Poles

Student lecture notes — FPE 8th ed., Sections 5.1–5.2; selected examples from §5.3

A gain changes every closed-loop pole at once. The root locus puts those changes on one picture, so that we can ask which responses feedback can produce before choosing a gain.

Prerequisites: Pole locations and response, Routh’s criterion, and feedback equations. We use complex numbers as vectors; no complex integration is needed.

Sources: Franklin, Powell & Emami-Naeini (FPE), Feedback Control of Dynamic Systems, 8th ed., §§5.1–5.3, checked against the corresponding sections of the 7th ed. The lecture follows the book’s order: §5.1 (the motor, Example 5.1), §5.2 (Definitions I and II, the trial point of Eq. (5.18) and Fig. 5.4, Rules 1–5 on the running example of Eq. (5.20), the summary of §5.2.2, and gain selection in §5.2.3), and the satellite of §5.3. The damping-parameter locus in §8 is Example 5.2 of the 8th ed.; the 7th ed. presents it in §5.1 without an example number. Nise, Control Systems Engineering, 7th ed., §§8.2–8.5, supplements the construction procedure. Åström & Murray, Feedback Systems, 2nd ed., §12.5, supplies the repeated-root viewpoint. Examples labelled [course example] are our own, not numbered textbook examples. Figures are recomputed from the stated models.

Learning objectives

  1. Put a characteristic equation in root-locus form, a(s)+Kb(s)=0 or 1+KL0​(s)=0.
  2. State Definitions I and II of the root locus and test a trial point with the phase condition.
  3. State and justify FPE’s Rules 1–5, and apply them to sketch a locus.
  4. Use the magnitude condition to select the gain that places a root at a chosen point on the locus.
  5. Find imaginary-axis crossings and the stable gain interval.
  6. Explain what a root-locus plot does and does not say about a closed loop.

Negative feedback gives 1+L=0. In the core rules, a and b are real, monic polynomials with no common factor, n>m, and any positive fixed scale factor has been absorbed into K. Monic means that the highest-power coefficient is one. A negative scale factor must remain explicit (§8). Unity feedback means H=1. Signals and motor gains are normalized; time is in seconds and pole coordinates are in s−1.

Notation bridge: FPE writes 1+KL(s)=0 with the gain omitted from its L. Our L0​ is FPE’s L; our L retains the meaning used in the earlier course notes. FPE’s asymptote center α is our σa​; we reserve α for the damping-ray parameter in Lecture II.

Where we are, and where this lecture goes

In Chapter 4 we built proportional, PI, and PID controllers and watched the closed-loop poles move as we turned the gains. For a second-order loop we could follow them with the quadratic formula. For anything higher we had only Routh’s criterion, which answers “stable or not?” for a given gain but says nothing about where the poles are or how fast the response will be. That gap is the subject of the next four lectures.

The idea is due to W. R. Evans, who in 1948 proposed plotting every root of the characteristic equation as the gain sweeps from zero to infinity, then reading the design off the picture. His rules let an engineer sketch the plot by hand, long before software could draw it. Today MATLAB draws it in one line, so the point of learning the rules is different: they tell you what the picture means, let you predict how adding a controller pole or zero will reshape it, and let you catch a plot that is wrong. FPE puts it bluntly: it is easy to get a constant wrong or leave out a term, and a rough hand sketch is how you notice.

The plan for this lecture follows FPE §§5.1–5.2:

  1. Start with the motor, where the quadratic formula draws the whole picture (§1, FPE §5.1).
  2. Define the root locus twice: once by the characteristic equation and once by a condition on phase, and test a trial point (§2, FPE §5.2).
  3. State Evans’s five rules, each justified from the phase condition and applied to FPE’s running example (§3, FPE §5.2.1), then collect them in one list (§4, FPE §5.2.2).
  4. Use the magnitude condition to select the gain that puts a root at a chosen point (§5, FPE §5.2.3).
  5. Sketch the course cubic from start to finish by the rules and find the gain at which it goes unstable (§6), then summarize the procedure (§7).

Lecture II uses the finished picture to choose a gain against specifications. Lectures III and IV reshape the picture with controller dynamics.

1. Root locus of a basic feedback system

With H=1,

T(s)=RY​=1+Dc​GDc​G​,1+KL0​(s)=0⟺a(s)+Kb(s)=0.

FPE calls these the root-locus form (or Evans form) of the characteristic equation; a third version, L0​(s)=−1/K, is the one the next section builds on. The root locus is the set of roots of this polynomial for all permitted K. A plotted point is a possible closed-loop pole. A particular gain selects all n roots together, not one independently chosen point on each branch.

At K=0 the roots are those of a; these are the branch starting points. With Dc​=K, the reference-to-output transfer function vanishes identically at K=0, so endpoints are best understood as characteristic roots or limits as K→0+. More generally, cancelling a factor in Y/R can hide a mode. Check the physical interconnection and internal stability before discarding it.

Motor position: FPE Example 5.1

The normalized motor model is G=A/[s(s+c)]. Set c=1 and vary A=K, with unit controller and sensor. Equivalently, use G=1/[s(s+1)], Dc​=K:

s(s+1)+K=0,s1,2​=−21​±41​−K​.

Increasing gain beyond 1/4 raises the oscillation frequency but leaves the exponential decay rate at 0.5 s−1. This is the geometric version of the limitation of proportional control from Chapter 4.

Now put the table on the s-plane. The left panel of the figure below plots every root in the table as K grows. At K=0 the two roots sit on the open-loop poles at 0 and −1. As K increases they move toward each other along the real axis, meet at −1/2 when K=1/4, and then leave the axis as a conjugate pair travelling straight up and down. The arrows show the direction of increasing gain. This picture is the root locus of the motor: every closed-loop pole the motor can have under proportional control lies on these two branches, and no gain can produce a pole anywhere else.

Read the design limitation directly from the geometry. The complex branches are a vertical line at ℜs=−1/2. Whatever gain we choose, the decay rate of the oscillatory response is fixed at 0.5 s−1; the gain only slides the poles up and down that line. Chapter 4 found this by algebra. The locus shows it at a glance, and it will show the analogous limitation for plants where the algebra is not available.

The right panel previews §6: a third-order plant whose locus bends into the right half-plane. For a quadratic we can solve for the roots and plot them directly. For that plant, and for anything of higher order, there is no formula, so we need a way to find the branches without solving for the roots. That is the job of the next section.

2. Guidelines for determining a root locus: two definitions

FPE defines the locus twice. The first definition is the one we have been using.

Definition I. The root locus is the set of values of s for which 1+KL0​(s)=0 is satisfied as the real parameter K varies from 0 to +∞. Typically 1+KL0​(s)=0 is the characteristic equation of the system, and in that case the roots on the locus are the closed-loop poles of that system.

Here is the reframing that makes the method work for any order. Instead of asking “where are the roots?”, pick a trial point s in the plane and ask “is this point a closed-loop pole for some positive gain?” Write the equation as L0​(s)=−1/K. If K is real and positive, then L0​(s) must be a negative real number: a complex number whose phase is 180∘, or 180∘ plus any multiple of 360∘. Its magnitude can be anything, because K is free to scale it. That gives the second definition.

Definition II. The root locus of L0​(s) is the set of points in the s-plane where the phase of L0​(s) is 180∘. If ψi​ is the angle from zero zi​ to the test point and ϕi​ is the angle from pole pi​ to the test point, then the locus is the set of points where, for some integer l, the phase condition below holds.

i∑​ψi​−i∑​ϕi​=180∘+360∘(l−1)​

The immense merit of Definition II, in FPE’s words, is that solving a high-order polynomial by hand is very difficult, while computing the phase of a transfer function is easy. Factoring a and b,

L0​(s)=∏i=1n​(s−pi​)∏i=1m​(s−zi​)​,argL0​(s)=i=1∑m​arg(s−zi​)−i=1∑n​arg(s−pi​).

Each factor s−pi​ or s−zi​ is itself a complex number with a simple picture. Stand at the trial point s and draw an arrow to it from each open-loop pole and zero. The arrow from pi​ is the vector s−pi​: its length is ∣s−pi​∣ and its angle, measured counterclockwise from the positive real direction, is ϕi​. The angle condition then says: add up the angles of the arrows from the zeros, subtract the angles of the arrows from the poles, and the total must be an odd multiple of 180∘. Use a quadrant-aware angle: the vector −1+j has angle 135∘, not −45∘. Angles differing by 360∘ represent the same direction.

The usual case, K>0, is called the positive or 180∘ locus. When K is real and negative, L0​(s) must be real and positive, with phase 0∘; that is the negative or 0∘ locus, taken up in §8.

Testing a trial point: FPE Eq. (5.18) and Fig. 5.4

FPE’s first illustration is

L0​(s)=s(s+5)[(s+2)2+4]s+1​,

with poles at 0, −5, and −2±2j and a zero at −1. Is the test point s0​=−1+2j on the locus for some K? Draw the five arrows (left panel of the figure below). The arrow from the zero at −1 is vertical, so ψ1​=90∘. From the pole at 0 the arrow −1+2j has ϕ1​=116.6∘; from −5 the arrow 4+2j has ϕ2​=26.6∘; from −2+2j the arrow 1+0j has ϕ3​=0∘; from −2−2j the arrow 1+4j has ϕ4​=76.0∘. Then

∑ψi​−∑ϕi​=90∘−116.6∘−26.6∘−0∘−76.0∘=−129.1∘.

(FPE rounds the individual angles first and prints −129.2∘.) The phase is not 180∘, so s0​ is not on the locus, and we would have to pick another point and try again. Measuring phase at one point is easy; measuring it at every point in the plane is hardly practical. That is why Evans’s rules exist: they say where to look.

A point that passes: the motor

For the motor at sd​=−1/2+j3​/2, the arrows from the poles at 0 and −1 have angles ϕ1​=120∘ and ϕ2​=60∘. There are no zeros, so ∑ψi​−∑ϕi​=−180∘, and the point is on the locus. Direct substitution confirms it: sd​(sd​+1)=−1, so K=1 places a root exactly there. The gain is the subject of §5; for now the lesson is that the angle test, not the magnitude, decides membership. A magnitude calculation alone would assign a positive number to any trial point, even one that fails the angle test.

Notice what has changed. The angle test never asks us to solve a polynomial. It depends only on the geometry of the open-loop pole-zero pattern relative to the trial point. That is why the method scales to high order, and it is also why adding a controller pole or zero, which adds one more arrow, has a predictable effect on the picture. Lectures III and IV are built on exactly that observation.

3. Rules for determining a positive (180°) root locus

Evans’s rules are the phase condition applied to special families of points, where the answer can be written down once and for all: points on the real axis, points very far away, and points very close to a pole or zero. FPE’s advice: the first three rules are simple to remember and essential for any reasonable sketch; the last two are used occasionally. We state each rule as FPE does, give its one-paragraph reason, and apply it to FPE’s running example,

L0​(s)=s[(s+4)2+16]1​=s(s2+8s+32)1​,poles 0, −4±4j,n=3, m=0.

The right panel of the figure above is this locus, built up rule by rule.

RULE 1. The n branches of the locus start at the poles of L0​(s), and m of these branches end on the zeros of L0​(s).

From a(s)+Kb(s)=0: at K=0 the equation reduces to a(s)=0, whose roots are the poles. As K→∞, either b(s)=0 or s→∞. There are m zeros where b(s)=0, so m branches can end there; the case s→∞ is Rule 3. Because the coefficients are real, the locus is symmetric about the real axis.

Example: three branches start at 0, −4+4j, and −4−4j. There are no finite zeros, so none of them ends at a finite point.

RULE 2. The loci are on the real axis to the left of an odd number of poles and zeros.

Take a test point on the real axis. The two angles from a complex-conjugate pair of poles cancel each other, as would the angles from a conjugate pair of zeros. The angle from a real pole or zero is 0∘ if the test point is to its right and 180∘ if the test point is to its left. For the total to be 180∘+360∘(l−1), the test point must be to the left of an odd number of real-axis poles plus zeros. Count multiplicity; for parity, subtracting a pole angle and adding a zero angle have the same effect.

Example: the only real pole is at 0, so the locus occupies the entire negative real axis. For the motor, the segment (−1,0) has exactly one pole to its right.

RULE 3. For large s and K, n−m branches of the loci are asymptotic to lines at angles ϕl​ radiating out from the point σa​ on the real axis, where

ϕl​=n−m180∘+360∘(l−1)​,l=1,2,…,n−m,σa​=n−m∑pi​−∑zi​​.

As K→∞, L0​(s)=−1/K can be satisfied only if L0​(s)→0: either at the m zeros (Rule 1) or as s→∞, since n>m. The asymptotes describe how the remaining n−m roots go to infinity. Stand very far from the whole cluster of poles and zeros. Every arrow then has nearly the same angle, and the m zero arrows cancel m of the n pole arrows, leaving n−m pole arrows that all point the same way. From that vantage point the plant looks like

1+(s−σa​)n−mK​=0,

a stack of n−m identical poles at a single point σa​. The phase of that simple system is 180∘ when its n−m equal angles sum to 180∘+360∘(l−1), which is the formula for ϕl​. The center σa​ comes from a property of polynomials: the coefficient of sn−1 in a monic polynomial is the negative sum of its roots. If m<n−1, that coefficient of a(s)+Kb(s) does not depend on K, so the sum of the closed-loop roots equals the sum of the open-loop poles at every gain. At large K, m roots sit at the zeros and n−m sit on the asymptotic system whose poles sum to (n−m)σa​; equating the two sums gives σa​. Imaginary parts cancel in both sums, so σa​ is real: a center of mass of the poles, with the zeros counted negatively.

Example: n−m=3, so ϕl​=60∘,180∘,300∘ (that is, ±60∘ and 180∘), and

σa​=30+(−4+4j)+(−4−4j)​=−38​=−2.67.

The ±60∘ asymptotes cross the imaginary axis at ±(8/3)tan60∘=±4.62j. The 180∘ asymptote is the negative real axis already found by Rule 2. For the motor, n−m=2 gives ±90∘ from σa​=(0−1)/2=−1/2: the vertical line through −1/2, exactly where the quadratic formula put the complex branches.

Asymptotes describe the far-away branches, not necessarily the locus near the plant poles. They intersect at σa​, which need not itself be a point on the locus.

RULE 4. The angle of departure of a branch of the locus from a single pole is

ϕdep​=i∑​ψi​−i=dep∑​ϕi​−180∘,

where ∑ϕi​ is the sum of the angles to the remaining poles and ∑ψi​ the sum of the angles to all the zeros. For a pole of multiplicity q,

qϕl,dep​=i∑​ψi​−i=dep∑​ϕi​−180∘−360∘(l−1),l=1,2,…,q,

and the angle(s) of arrival at a zero of multiplicity q are

qψl,arr​=i∑​ϕi​−i=arr∑​ψi​+180∘+360∘(l−1),l=1,2,…,q.

Take a test point a very small distance from the pole in question. The arrows from every other pole and zero are essentially the arrows to that pole itself, whose angles we can compute. Only the short arrow from the pole has an unknown angle, and transposing everything else in the phase condition to the right-hand side solves for it. For a multiple pole the unknown angle is counted q times, which is why l takes q values and q branches depart. The same argument at large K gives the arrival angle at a zero. The arrival angle is the angle of the vector from the zero to nearby locus points; the direction of motion toward the zero is opposite that ray.

Example: at the pole −4+4j, the arrow from −4−4j is vertical, ϕ2​=90∘, and the arrow from 0 is −4+4j, ϕ3​=135∘. There are no zeros, so

ϕdep​=−90∘−135∘−180∘=−405∘=−45∘.

By conjugate symmetry the branch from −4−4j departs at +45∘. FPE notes that this rule is particularly useful when a pole lies near the imaginary axis, because it shows whether the branch starts off toward the stable left half-plane or toward the unstable right half-plane.

[Course example] For L0​=1/[(s+4)(s2+2s+5)], the upper pole is −1+2j. The arrows from the other poles have angles 90∘ and tan−1(2/3)=33.69∘, so ϕdep​=−90∘−33.69∘−180∘=−303.69∘=56.31∘. Its branch initially moves rightward: a lightly damped mechanical mode, such as a flexible link or a compliant gearbox, need not initially move deeper into the left half-plane under feedback.

Repeated poles: for the rigid satellite model L0​=1/s2 of FPE §5.3, q=2 and there are no other poles or zeros, so 2ϕl,dep​=−180∘−360∘(l−1) gives ϕdep​=±90∘. Equivalently s2=−K. Proportional control of a double integrator creates oscillation but no damping. The same local argument appears in Åström–Murray §12.5: near a pole of multiplicity q, (s−p)q≈−Kb(p)/a(p), and the q rays are separated by 360∘/q.

RULE 5. The locus can have multiple roots at points on the locus of multiplicity q. The branches will approach a point of q roots at angles separated by

q180∘+360∘(l−1)​

and will depart at angles with the same separation, forming an array of 2q rays equally spaced. If the point is on the real axis, the orientation of this array is given by the real-axis rule. If the point is in the complex plane, the angle-of-departure rule must be applied.

Watch the motor’s two real roots again. One leaves the pole at 0 moving left; the other leaves −1 moving right. They cannot pass through each other as distinct real roots: at the meeting point the characteristic polynomial has a double root, and beyond it the roots must become a complex-conjugate pair, because the coefficients are real. FPE’s Fig. 5.2 shows exactly this: at K=1/4 there are two roots at s=−1/2, the horizontal branches come together, and the vertical branches break away. The locus arrives at 0∘ and 180∘ and departs at +90∘ and −90∘: four rays, q=2, separated by 90∘. The meeting point is a breakaway point; the reverse event, a complex pair landing on the axis and splitting into two real roots, is a break-in point. FPE computes the departure angles from such a point with a continuation locus: restart the locus at the multiple root with K=K1​+K2​ and apply Rule 4 to the repeated pole.

To find the points, use the gain along the real axis,

K(s)=−b(s)a(s)​.

A multiple closed-loop root satisfies both a+Kb=0 and a′+Kb′=0. Eliminating K gives

a′b−ab′=0​ordsdK​=0,

provided b(s)=0. These are candidates. Keep only real candidates on an allowed real-axis segment with K>0. There is a useful picture behind the derivative. On the motor’s segment (−1,0), K(s) is zero at each pole and positive in between, so it rises from one end, peaks, and falls to the other. Two roots moving inward from the ends reach the same gain only at the peak, so the breakaway is where K(s) is maximum on the segment. On a segment between two zeros, K is infinite at both ends and the break-in is at its minimum.

Example: for the motor, K=−s2−s, so dK/ds=−2s−1=0 gives s=−1/2 at K=1/4. The FPE running example has no real-axis breakaway: its real-axis branch runs from the pole at 0 out to −∞ alone, and its complex branches never touch the axis.

Which rules matter most. FPE’s closing remark on §5.2.1 is worth repeating. It is usually sufficient to draw the locus using only Rules 1 to 3, which should be memorized. Rule 4 is sometimes useful to understand how a branch departs, especially from a pole near the imaginary axis. Rule 5 helps interpret computer plots and, in later sections, explains how a locus changes qualitatively as a pole or zero is moved. The accurate curves come from MATLAB:

s = tf('s');
sysL = 1/(s*((s+4)^2+16));      % FPE Eq. (5.20)
rlocus(sysL); grid on

4. Summary of the rules for determining a root locus

This is FPE §5.2.2, in our notation.

  1. RULE 1. The n branches of the locus start at the poles of L0​(s), and m branches end on the zeros of L0​(s).
  2. RULE 2. The loci are on the real axis to the left of an odd number of poles and zeros.
  3. RULE 3. For large s and K, n−m branches of the loci are asymptotic to lines at angles ϕl​ radiating out from the center point σa​ on the real axis, where ϕl​=n−m180∘+360∘(l−1)​,l=1,2,…,n−m,σa​=n−m∑pi​−∑zi​​.
  4. RULE 4. The angle(s) of departure of a branch of the locus from a pole of multiplicity q are given by qϕl,dep​=∑ψi​−i=dep∑​ϕi​−180∘−360∘(l−1), where l=1,2,…,q, and the angle(s) of arrival of a branch at a zero of multiplicity q are given by qψl,arr​=∑ϕi​−i=arr∑​ψi​+180∘+360∘(l−1).
  5. RULE 5. The locus can have multiple roots at points on the locus of multiplicity q. The branches approach a point of q roots at angles separated by [180∘+360∘(l−1)]/q and depart at angles with the same separation, forming an array of 2q rays equally spaced. If the point is on the real axis, the orientation of this array is given by the real-axis rule. If the point is in the complex plane, the angle-of-departure rule must be applied.

Two practical additions that the rules do not state: multiple roots on the real axis are found from dK/ds=0 on an allowed segment, and imaginary-axis crossings are found from Routh’s criterion or by substituting s=jω (§6).

5. Selecting the parameter value

The positive locus is a plot of all possible root locations for some positive K. Design means choosing a particular K. Definition II used only the phase of L0​; if a root is actually to sit at a chosen point on the locus, the magnitude condition must hold as well. From L0​(s)=−1/K,

K=∣L0​(s)∣1​=∏i​∣s−zi​∣∏i​∣s−pi​∣​​.

This has a graphical reading: on a plot with equal scales on the two axes, K is the product of the lengths of the arrows from the poles divided by the product of the lengths of the arrows from the zeros. FPE remarks that nobody does this by hand to finish a design; MATLAB’s rlocfind or rltool returns the gain at a clicked point. The value of the hand calculation is as a sanity check on the computer’s answer.

FPE’s example: a damping ratio of 0.5 on the Eq. (5.20) locus

On the right panel of the §2 figure, the dotted rays make angle cos−10.5=60∘ with the negative real axis, so every point on them has ζ=0.5. Suppose we want the complex pair of closed-loop poles there. The locus crosses the upper ray at s0​=−2+23​j≈−2+3.46j. Reading lengths off the figure as FPE does, ∣s0​∣≈4.0, ∣s0​−(−4+4j)∣≈2.1, and ∣s0​−(−4−4j)∣≈7.7, so K≈4.0(2.1)(7.7)≈65. The exact lengths are 4, 4+(23​−4)2​, and 4+(23​+4)2​, whose product is exactly 64. The graphical estimate is good to about two percent.

Where is the third root? Rule 3’s sum-of-roots property answers without searching: the roots sum to −8 at every gain, the pair sums to −4, so the third root is −4. Indeed

s3+8s2+32s+64=(s+4)(s2+4s+16).

The design is not finished when the poles are placed. This loop has one integrator, so it is Type 1, and with K=64 its velocity constant is

Kv​=s→0lim​sKL0​(s)=32K​=2 s−1.

If the dynamic response implied by the root locations and the steady-state accuracy implied by Kv​ are both acceptable, gain selection alone completes the design. If no value of K satisfies all the constraints, which FPE notes is the typical case, the controller must be given dynamics. Lecture II makes the first check systematic; Lectures III and IV supply the dynamics.

For the motor, the magnitude condition at sd​=−1/2+j3​/2 gives K=∣sd​∣∣sd​+1∣=1, agreeing with the direct substitution in §2.

6. A complete sketch by the rules [course example]

Now apply the rules in order to the cubic that Lecture II will design against. Think of it as the motor with one more lag: an amplifier, a filtered velocity sensor, or a compliant coupling each add a pole, and here that pole sits at −4 with the other poles at 0 and −2:

L0​(s)=s(s+2)(s+4)1​,P(s,K)=s3+6s2+8s+K.

RULE 1. The poles are 0,−2,−4 and there are no finite zeros, so three branches start at the poles and all three go to infinity.

RULE 2. The real-axis segment (−2,0) has one pole to its right and is on the locus; so is (−∞,−4), with three poles to its right. The segment (−4,−2) has two poles to its right and is not on the locus.

RULE 3. With n−m=3,

σa​=30−2−4​=−2,ϕl​=60∘,180∘,300∘.

Already the sketch tells the main story. Two of the three far branches leave at ±60∘ from the center −2, and a ray at 60∘ from a point on the negative real axis must eventually cross into the right half-plane. So the closed loop becomes unstable at some large enough gain. The remaining questions are where the two roots from 0 and −2 leave the axis, and at what gain the crossing happens.

RULE 4. All poles are real and simple; the branches leave the real poles along the real axis in the directions Rule 2 allows.

RULE 5. The two roots starting at 0 and −2 move toward each other and must leave the axis between them. The stationary-gain equation is

3s2+12s+8=0⟹s=−2±323​​.

Only s=−0.8453 lies on a positive-gain segment. It gives K=163​/9≈3.0792. The other candidate, −3.155, lies on (−4,−2) and gives negative gain; it is rejected. Two branches arrive at −0.8453 along the axis and depart at ±90∘.

Crossing the imaginary axis. The pair leaves the axis at −0.845 and curves outward toward the ±60∘ asymptotes. To find the gain at which it reaches the imaginary axis we do not need the plot at all: that is precisely the stability boundary Routh’s criterion locates.

s3s2s1s0​16(48−K)/6K​8K00​⟹0<K<48​.

At K=48, the auxiliary polynomial is 6s2+48, giving s=±j8​; the remaining root is −6. Indeed,

s3+6s2+8s+48=(s+6)(s2+8).

The imaginary roots describe sustained oscillations in the zero-input linear model. This boundary is not BIBO stable. Above it two branches enter the RHP. Thus more gain eventually destabilizes this plant, even though more gain always keeps the earlier motor model stable.

Compare the two panels of the figure in §1 once more. The motor’s branches turn vertical and never cross the axis. Adding one pole at −4 pulls the asymptotes from ±90∘ to ±60∘, and that is enough to bend the branches into the right half-plane. This is the lesson that generalizes. Each additional excess pole adds one more asymptote direction, and the outermost pair leans further toward the right half-plane: ±90∘ for two excess poles, ±60∘ for three, ±45∘ for four. Extra lags push a locus toward instability, and the locus shows it before any gain is chosen.

7. Sketch first, compute second

Here is the whole procedure, in the order we used it on the cubic.

  1. Derive the characteristic polynomial and identify the varied parameter; put it in root-locus form.
  2. Mark poles and zeros; apply Rules 1–3 for endpoints, real-axis segments, and asymptotes.
  3. Apply Rule 4 at any complex or repeated pole, and Rule 5 with dK/ds=0 for real-axis breakaway and break-in points.
  4. Use Routh or s=jω to locate crossings.
  5. Compute roots at a few gains, including every root at each gain.
  6. Add arrows for increasing gain. Select K by the magnitude condition, then verify any proposed design in the time domain.

A numerical plot fills in the curves. It does not replace the sign, stability, or cancellation checks.

s = tf('s');                      % Control System Toolbox
L0 = 1/(s*(s+2)*(s+4));
rlocus(L0); grid on
K = 48;
roots([1 6 8 K])                  % Characteristic roots at this gain

8. Extensions: another parameter or another sign

FPE’s Example 5.2 (8th ed.; §5.1 of the 7th ed.) varies a plant pole rather than controller gain. For s2+cs+A=0, fixed A>0 and variable c≥0 give

1+cs2+As​=0.

That is a different locus, with starting poles ±jA​ and a zero at zero. It describes increasing physical damping, not increasing motor gain. The root-locus method applies to any real parameter that enters the characteristic equation linearly.

For negative K, or positive feedback written 1−KL0​=0, the angle condition is 0∘ modulo 360∘ (FPE §5.6.1). Re-derive the real-axis and asymptote rules for that sign: the locus lies to the left of an even number of poles and zeros, and the asymptote angles become 360∘(l−1)/(n−m).

[Course example] L0​=(1−s)/[s(s+2)] has a positive DC-direction numerator but a negative leading coefficient. Do not replace 1−s by s−1 without retaining the minus sign. The closed-loop polynomial is

s2+(2−K)s+K,

stable only for 0<K<2. As K→∞, one root approaches the RHP zero at +1. Endpoint reasoning identifies a high-gain limitation before detailed design.

What to take away

  • The root locus is the set of all closed-loop poles for all gains (Definition I). A gain picks one point on every branch at once.
  • A point is on the locus when the phase of L0​ there is 180∘ (Definition II): the zero-arrow angles minus the pole-arrow angles. No polynomial is solved.
  • Rules 1–5 are the phase condition applied to special points. Rules 1–3 (endpoints, real axis, asymptotes) draw almost everything and should be memorized; Rule 4 (departure) matters near the imaginary axis; Rule 5 (multiple roots) explains breakaway and break-in.
  • The magnitude condition K=1/∣L0​(s)∣ selects the gain for a chosen point, and the sum of the roots locates the remaining ones.
  • Extra poles spread the asymptotes and bend branches toward the right half-plane; the cubic became gain-limited at K=48, while FPE’s running example is limited at K=256.
  • The plot tells you where the poles can go, and the magnitude condition which gain puts them there. Whether that gain meets the specifications is Lecture II.
