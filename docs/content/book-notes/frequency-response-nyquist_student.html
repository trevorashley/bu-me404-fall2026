The Nyquist Stability Criterion

Franklin, Powell, and Emami-Naeini (FPE), Feedback Control of Dynamic Systems, 8th ed., §6.3, Examples 6.8–6.11. The corresponding 7th-edition material is on pp. 333–348. For a second treatment of the contour orientation and sign convention, see Ogata, Modern Control Engineering, 5th ed., §7-5, and Åström and Murray, Feedback Systems, 2nd ed., §10.2.

Prerequisites. Review root-locus vector angles, the Routh test, and neutral stability.

Objectives. Trace a directed path and its image, use their angle changes to count unstable closed-loop poles, and complete the path correctly for a plant with an integrator. By the last example, you should be able to explain why a zero on the imaginary axis behaves differently from a pole there.

Throughout, j2=−1, s=σ+jω, and ω is angular frequency in rad/s. The examples use real, rational transfer functions and unity negative feedback. The full loop is L=KG, with the scalar controller gain K included once.

1. Count turns by following vectors

In the preceding note, L(jω)=−1 located a stability boundary. On which side of that gain does a transient grow? Nyquist’s idea is to count the characteristic roots in the right half-plane (RHP) using values of the loop on its boundary.

A contour is a directed closed path. Mark a moving point s on a contour, evaluate a function F(s) there, and put a dot at the resulting complex value in a second plane. Moving the two dots together traces the image of the contour. Its net number of turns around a target is its winding number with a specified choice of positive direction. A turn in one direction cancels a turn in the other.

To see what controls the number of turns, factor a rational function with no pole or zero on the path:

F(s)=c∏k​(s−pk​)∏i​(s−zi​)​,argF(s)=argc+i∑​arg(s−zi​)−k∑​arg(s−pk​).

Here s−zi​ is the vector pointing from the zero to the moving point. Follow its angle continuously, allowing it to pass through a full revolution. For one clockwise trip around a simple contour, a vector anchored inside sweeps through −2π radians. A vector anchored outside swings back to its initial direction with zero net rotation. The same geometry applies to a pole vector, but its angle is subtracted. With Z enclosed zeros and P enclosed poles, counting repeated factors separately,

ΔargF=−2πZ+2πP,N=−2πΔargF​=Z−P.

We have defined clockwise image turns as positive. One enclosed pole by itself therefore gives N=−1. The schematic below evaluates F=(s−z)/(s−p) on the same clockwise circle in each row. Open circles mark zeros and crosses mark poles.

In the top row both vector angles return with zero net rotation. In the bottom row only the pole vector makes a full turn, and the image circles the origin counterclockwise. An exactly canceled pole and zero likewise contribute zero net angle. A physical mode hidden by cancellation still requires an internal stability check.

Watch out. Track the minus sign on every denominator angle. A clockwise circuit around a pole can produce a counterclockwise circuit in the image.

2. Turn the argument principle into a feedback test

Write G=b/a, with a,b coprime (no common factor). Assume a well-posed interconnection. From Y=L(R−Y),

T=1+LL​=a+KbKb​,F=1+L=aa+Kb​.

For K=0, the zeros of F are the characteristic roots, and its poles are the open-loop poles. Traverse the imaginary axis upward from −jR to +jR, then close the path through the right half-plane on s=Rejθ, with θ decreasing from π/2 to −π/2. Take R large enough to include all finite RHP poles and zeros of F. This is a clockwise contour. Adding 1 shifts the entire image one unit right, so F about 0 and L about −1 have the same turns. The figure illustrates that translation for one finite contour.

The cross moves with the curve. A turn around −1 in the lower left remains a turn around 0 in the lower right. For several nonzero real gains it is convenient to draw G about −1/K, as in all four examples. Multiplication by K scales the curve and target together. For K<0 it also rotates them by 180∘, preserving the direction of traversal.

The imaginary-axis segment is available from frequency response. Real coefficients give G(−jω)=G(jω)​. Draw the reflected branch running from large negative frequency toward zero, then follow the positive branch outward. For a strictly proper model with G(s)∼c/sr, r>0, the outer arc shrinks to the origin as R grows. For equal numerator and denominator degree, it shrinks to the ratio of leading coefficients.

An imaginary-axis pole makes direct evaluation there impossible. Bypass it with a small semicircle into the RHP, leaving the pole outside the enclosed region. For a pole of order m at zero, set s=ϵejθ with θ increasing from −π/2 to +π/2. Locally,

G(s)∼c/sm=cϵ−me−jmθ.

The mapped arc has large radius and turns clockwise by mπ radians. Its midpoint lies in the direction of c. At another boundary pole use the same construction centered at that pole.

Watch out. Reflecting a measured positive-frequency trace supplies the negative-frequency branch. The mapped indentation is a separate piece needed to close the curve.

Apply FPE’s procedure in this order:

  1. Plot the image of the complete contour, including detours and the outer closure.
  2. Count its net clockwise encirclements N of −1 for L (or −1/K for G).
  3. Count the open-loop poles P strictly inside the RHP contour.
  4. Compute Z=N+P. Asymptotic stability requires Z=0, no characteristic roots on the imaginary axis, and no hidden nondecaying modes.

If the curve reaches its target, the vector to the curve has zero length there and its angle is undefined. Find boundary poles from a+Kb=0 directly. To read a regular count from a drawing, extend a ray left from the target. Each upward crossing of that ray contributes +1 (as at the left edge of a clockwise circle), each downward crossing contributes −1. Count each traversal, including repeated passages through the same plotted point.

3. Example 6.8 — A stable second-order open loop

Take G=1/(s+1)2. Multiply the numerator and denominator of 1/(1+jω)2 by (1−jω)2. This gives

G(jω)=(1+ω2)21−ω2−2jω​,∣G∣=1+ω21​,argG=−2tan−1ω.

On the positive-frequency branch, G starts at 1, passes through −j/2 at 1 rad/s, and approaches zero from below. The Bode panels give the radius and angle of each matching point in the complex plane. Frequencies 0 and ∞ appear as endpoints only in the complex plane because the Bode frequency axis is logarithmic.

The solid branch stays below the real axis for every finite ω>0 and the reflected branch stays above. There is no negative real-axis crossing, so for K>0 the image encloses no negative target. Both plant poles are at −1, hence P=N=Z=0. For K=−0.5 the target is 2, outside; for K=−2 it is 0.5, inside the clockwise loop. In general −1<K<0 gives −1/K>1 and N=Z=0, while K<−1 gives 0<−1/K<1 and N=Z=1. At K=−1 the target is the starting value G(0)=1.

Check both gain signs by solving (s+1)2+K=0. Positive gain gives s=−1±jK​; negative gain gives s=−1±−K​. The latter are the 0∘ root-locus branches, since a negative gain supplies the half-turn in KG=−1. Compare their directions below.

The squares at 0,−2 mark the boundary at K=−1. At K=0, use the polynomial directly: both internal plant poles remain at −1, even though T reduces to zero. The full stable range is K>−1.

4. Example 6.9 — An integrator in the third-order loop

Return to G=1/[s(s+1)2] from the neutral-stability note. On the imaginary axis the denominator is D=−2ω2+jω(1−ω2). Using 1/D=D/∣D∣2 and ∣D∣2=ω2(1+ω2)2 gives

ℜG=(1+ω2)2−2​,ℑG=ω(1+ω2)2ω2−1​.

The magnitudes of the three denominator factors multiply and their angles add, so ∣G∣=1/[ω(1+ω2)] and argG=−90∘−2tan−1ω for ω>0. At small positive frequency the real part tends to −2 and the imaginary part behaves like −1/ω; at 1 rad/s it crosses the real axis at −1/2. Above 1 rad/s the imaginary part is positive and the branch returns to the origin from above. Follow the diamond in the two Bode panels into the complex plane.

The diamond has radius 0.5 and angle −180∘. The complex-plane detail enlarges the crossing; the low-frequency ends extend beyond this view. To connect them, use G(s)∼1/s near the origin. The rightward indent maps from roughly +j/ϵ through the positive real side to −j/ϵ. The next figure shows the entire finite contour and the missing arc in its image.

The pole at the origin lies outside, so P=0. The two frequency branches each cross the real axis upward at −1/2 as the contour is traversed. A target left of that point (for instance −2 at K=0.5) has N=0. A target between −1/2 and zero (such as −0.25 at K=4) has N=2. For a positive target (K<0), use a ray extending right: only the large right arc crosses it, moving downward, giving one clockwise turn. Thus Z=0 for 0<K<2, Z=2 for K>2, and Z=1 for K<0.

Independently, q=s(s+1)2+K=s3+2s2+s+K. Its Routh first column is 1,2,(2−K)/2,K. Its sign changes reproduce all three counts. At K=2, q=(s+2)(s2+1) and the image hits its target. At zero gain the integrator persists. The stable interval is 0<K<2.

5. Example 6.10 — Stabilizing an unstable open loop

Now take G=(s+1)/[s(s/10−1)]=10(s+1)/[s(s−10)]. The pole at +10 contributes P=1; the pole at zero requires an indent. Substitute s=jω and multiply by the conjugate of −ω2−10jω:

G(jω)=ω2(ω2+100)10(1+jω)(−ω2+10jω)​=ω2+100−110​+jω(ω2+100)10(10−ω2)​.

For ω>0 the imaginary part vanishes at ω=10​≈3.1623 rad/s, where G=−1. The positive-frequency phase starts near 90∘, passes through 180∘ and approaches 270∘; at high frequency G∼10/s approaches zero from below. This algebraic response is sufficient for Nyquist analysis. In a free-running open-loop experiment the unstable natural transient can grow through the sinusoidal output.

For the Bode plot, write G(jω)=−(1+jω)/[jω(1−jω/10)]. Taking factor magnitudes gives 101+ω2​/[ω100+ω2​]. Adding the numerator angles and subtracting the denominator angles gives 90∘+tan−1ω+tan−1(ω/10) on the plotted branch.

The Bode diamond and the negative real-axis crossing below correspond to the same frequency.

As frequency increases, the solid branch crosses downward at −1. The reflected branch crosses downward there too. This time G∼−1/s at the origin, so the indent runs from roughly −j/ϵ through the negative real side to +j/ϵ. Locate that extra upward crossing at the far left of the full image below.

For 0<K<1, the target lies left of −1. Only the large arc crosses the leftward ray, giving N=1 and Z=2. For K>1, the target is between −1 and zero. Both downward crossings now contribute too: N=1−2=−1 and Z=−1+1=0. For K<0, the target is positive and the image makes no turn about it, so N=0 and Z=1.

The characteristic polynomial checks the change of direction:

q=s(s−10)+10K(s+1)=s2+(10K−10)s+10K.

Both trailing coefficients are positive exactly when K>1, the second-order stability condition. For 0<K<1 the roots have positive sum and positive product, giving two RHP roots. At K=1 they are ±j10​. At K<0 their product is negative, so one is positive. At K=0 the internal poles are 0 and +10. The locus shows the positive-gain poles approaching the boundary from the unstable side.

The squares mark the crossing into the left half-plane as gain increases through 1. This time increasing gain stabilizes the system.

Why this matters. With one open-loop RHP pole, stability requires one net counterclockwise turn. A count of zero would leave one unstable closed-loop pole.

6. Example 6.11 — An imaginary-axis zero and a finite limit

Take G=(s2+3)/(s+1)2. The displayed model has order two, as identified in the 8th edition. The 7th-edition example calls it third-order. With s=jω the numerator is real. Multiplying by (1−jω)2/(1−jω)2 gives

ℜG=(1+ω2)2(ω2−3)(ω2−1)​,ℑG=(1+ω2)22ω(ω2−3)​.

The curve starts at G(0)=3. At ω=3​ the numerator vanishes: the image passes through the origin, where its phase is undefined. Because this is a zero of G, 1+KG=1 there, and the contour can run straight through that frequency. The magnitude is ∣3−ω2∣/(1+ω2). Below the zero the numerator is positive, and above it the negative numerator contributes 180∘. Thus, in degrees,

argG(jω)={−2tan−1ω,180∘−2tan−1ω,​0≤ω<3​,ω>3​.​

Since tan−13​=60∘, the one-sided limits are −120∘ and +60∘. The two leading coefficients equal 1, so the outer contour maps to 1 in the large-radius limit. The only real-axis points of the limiting image are 3, 0 and 1. Every positive gain puts the target on the negative real axis, with N=P=Z=0. As a check,

q=(s+1)2+K(s2+3)=(1+K)s2+2s+(1+3K).

All coefficients are positive for K>0, confirming stability throughout the positive-gain range. In the figure, compare the zero in the magnitude panel with the gap in the phase curve.

The diamond marks a true zero on a linear magnitude scale (−∞ dB). No phase is assigned to it. The downward triangle marks the finite high-frequency endpoint at 1. Neither feature requires the large mapped indentation used for an integrator.

Takeaway. With the RHP contour traversed clockwise, count clockwise turns of the full loop around −1 as positive. Then Z=N+P. The two integrator examples show why the side of the detour and the open-loop pole count both matter.
