Neutral Stability

Franklin, Powell, and Emami-Naeini, Feedback Control of Dynamic Systems, 8th ed., §6.2, Figs. 6.14–6.15, give the unnumbered example used throughout this note. The same example appears in the 7th ed., pp. 331–333. For the Routh test and its zero-row case, see also Nise, Control Systems Engineering, 7th ed., Ch. 6.

Prerequisites. Review root-locus construction, the Routh stability test, and Bode plot techniques.

Objectives. Use a Bode plot to find the gain and frequency at which a closed-loop pole pair reaches the imaginary axis. Determine which gains give decaying motion and explain what additional information a general stability test needs.

We assume a real, continuous-time linear system with unity negative feedback. The plant is G(s), the controller is a constant gain K, and the full loop is L(s)=KG(s). We start with K>0. Transfer responses assume zero initial conditions.

1. Evaluate the characteristic equation on the imaginary axis

The error is E=R−Y, so the output satisfies Y=KG(R−Y). Collecting the terms in Y gives (1+KG)Y=KGR. The reference-to-output transfer function is therefore

T(s)=R(s)Y(s)​=1+KG(s)KG(s)​.

At a closed-loop characteristic root where G is finite, 1+KG(s)=0. A pole s=σ+jω contributes a time dependence eσtejωt, where j2=−1. The real part σ sets the decay or growth rate. A simple conjugate pair at s=±jω0​ contributes a sinusoid of constant amplitude. To find such a boundary, evaluate the loop at s=jω0​ with ω0​>0:

L(jω0​)=−1⟺∣KG(jω0​)∣=1,argG(jω0​)=−180∘(mod360∘).

The point −1 is one unit from the origin on the negative real axis. Its angle can be written as either 180∘ or −180∘. A positive K scales the magnitude and preserves the phase. A negative K would add a half-turn.

Connection. Root locus varies K to follow a pole through the s-plane. A frequency sweep fixes σ=0 and looks along that axis for a point where the same characteristic equation holds.

2. Find the boundary for FPE’s third-order plant

For G(s)=1/[s(s+1)2], clearing the denominator of 1+KG(s) gives the closed-loop characteristic polynomial

q(s)=s(s+1)2+K=s3+2s2+s+K.

Substitute s=jω and use j2=−1:

q(jω)=(K−2ω2)+jω(1−ω2).

A complex number vanishes when both parts vanish. For ω>0, the imaginary part requires ω0​=1 rad/s. The real part then requires Kcrit​=2ω02​=2.

Now read those same two numbers from the loop frequency response. The denominator factor jω has magnitude ω and angle 90∘. Each copy of 1+jω has magnitude 1+ω2​ and angle tan−1ω (in degrees). Multiplying factors adds their angles, and taking the reciprocal reverses the sign. Thus

∣L(jω)∣=ω(1+ω2)K​,argL(jω)=−90∘−2tan−1ω.

The phase crossover is where the phase reaches −180∘. Here tan−1ω=45∘ gives ωpc​=1 rad/s. At that frequency (1+j)2=2j, so G(j)=1/[j(2j)]=−1/2. The loop has the required direction for every positive gain, but its magnitude K/2 reaches unity precisely at K=2. The phase condition found the frequency and the magnitude condition selected the gain, just as the two polynomial equations did.

3. Determine which side is stable

Use the Routh test to count roots in the right half-plane. The first two rows take alternating coefficients from q. The leading entry of the third row is (2⋅1−1⋅K)/2. For K=2 the resulting array is

s3s2s1s0​12(2−K)/2K​1K00​

All first-column entries are positive precisely when 0<K<2. For K>2, the signs +,+,−,+ give two right-half-plane poles. Extending the polynomial test to K<0, the signs +,+,+,− give one. At K=0, q=s(s+1)2 retains a pole at the origin. Setting the gain to zero disconnects the reference, but an initial displacement in the zero mode can persist.

At K=2, the s1 row vanishes. The s2 row supplies the auxiliary polynomial 2s2+2, whose roots are ±j. Factoring the original cubic gives q=(s+2)(s2+1), so the remaining mode decays as e−2t. The imaginary poles are simple. Free motion stays bounded, and an oscillatory component persists. This is neutral stability. Asymptotic stability requires all free motion to decay.

Watch out. Finding L(jω0​)=−1 locates a boundary. To identify the stable side, follow the poles or count them on each side of that gain.

4. Read the boundary in the time response

At K=2, a unit step R(s)=1/s gives Y(s)=2/[s(s+2)(s2+1)]. Write

Y(s)=sA​+s+2B​+s2+1Cs+D​.

Clearing the denominator and setting s=0,−2 gives A=1, B=−1/5. Matching the cubic and quadratic coefficients gives A+B+C=0 and 2A+2C+D=0, hence C=−4/5 and D=−2/5.

Y(s)=s1​−s+21/5​−s2+1(4/5)s+2/5​.

Using the transform pairs for the exponential, cosine, and sine gives, for t≥0,

y(t)=1−51​e−2t−54​cost−52​sint.

Once the exponential decays, the output oscillates about 1 with period 2π seconds. Read each step trace on its own axes. The windows span 50, 20, and 10 seconds so that each mode is visible.

The slow real pole at K=0.1 gives a gradual approach to 1. The pair at K=2 sustains the oscillation. At K=10 its positive real part produces an increasing envelope. Even at the neutral gain, a bounded sinusoidal input at 1 rad/s can produce unbounded output: its poles at ±j coincide with the closed-loop poles, introducing repeated poles and an oscillation whose amplitude grows with time.

For this plant with K>0, the phase crosses −180∘ once, and ∣L(jωpc​)∣=K/2<1 is equivalent to the stable interval. Other plants can become stable as gain increases, or have several crossovers. One magnitude reading cannot resolve those cases. The next note develops the Nyquist criterion, which combines the open-loop right-half-plane pole count with the signed number of turns the loop response makes around −1 along a complete contour.

Design implication. At the critical gain a disturbance can excite an oscillation that never dies away. A design that needs transients to settle must operate inside the stable range.

Takeaway. A simultaneous 0 dB and −180∘ crossing identifies an imaginary-axis characteristic root. For this cubic the crossing occurs at 1 rad/s and K=2, and the Routh count places the stable gains strictly between 0 and 2.
